ABSTRACT
Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable outcomes in various natural language processing
tasks. However, their application to the highly specialized field of law presents unique challenges. Legal
language, characterized by complex syntax, domain-specific terminology, and nuanced logical relationships,
poses significant hurdles for existing NLP models in accurately understanding and processing legal queries.
Furthermore, the sheer volume of legal documents complicates information retrieval and knowledge extraction,
making it difficult for models to pinpoint relevant legal articles and cases. Moreover, existing legal LLMs
often struggle to effectively handle colloquial user queries and lack efficient mechanisms for selecting the
most relevant demonstrations in In-Context Learning (ICL), hindering their ability to provide accurate and
comprehensive legal advice. In order to address these issues, we propose a novel prompting framework named
‘‘Collaborative Legal Experts’’ (CoLE). This framework draws inspiration from teamwork paradigms in realworld legal case processing. First, we design an intent identification module to analyze user queries for
identifying potential intents and law domains. Then, through two subsequent processes, potential background
information and the best demonstration are generated. Finally, we design a prompt generator to assemble
prompts generated from the previous steps. It combines with the LLMs to generate the final answer. Notably,
we find that the self-generated information by LLMs has a smaller gap when fused with LLMs. We evaluate
performance by integrating it with 7 general-purpose Chinese LLMs and comparing its performance against
8 specialized legal LLMs across 10 datasets, including Single-Choice, Multiple-Choice, and Question&Answer.
The results indicate that integrating with CoLE’s LLMs has the potential to significantly enhance performance
in the law field, particularly without the need for annotated datasets or model parameter updates. Moreover,
our proposed model outperforms all state-of-the-art LLMs in law. The code is available at https://github.com/
liboaccn/cole.

1. Introduction
With the increase of computing power and the amount of data, Artificial Intelligence (AI) technology develops rapidly [1–3]. Large Language Models (LLMs) [4–8] have made significant strides in the field of
Natural Language Processing (NLP). Moreover, some Chinese-oriented
LLMs [9–13] have enhanced Chinese understanding, often outperforming multilingual models in Chinese NLP tasks. Beyond demonstrating
exceptional performance across a range of standard tasks, there is
growing interest in the development of specialized LLMs for particular
domains, including education [14], healthcare [15,16], law [17–20]
and finance [21,22]. Recently, many works apply LLMs to the law
domain to solve some problems that are not well addressed by traditional methods [23–28]. Major advancements have been made in areas

such as legal prediction [23,24], interpretation of legal provisions [25],
dispute mediation [29], legal information extraction [26–28], and legal
reasoning [18,30–32]. As the population increases, so does the number
of judicial cases. Users can ask legal questions to the model and
receive responses and suggestions based on training data [33–35]. The
application of LLMs in the law field is expected to alleviate the problem
of ‘‘many cases and fewer people’’, thus improving the efficiency and
quality of the justice system [36,37]. However, applying LLMs to
the law field presents significant challenges. Legal language, with its
specialized terminology, complex sentence structures, and reliance on
precedent, poses a significant challenge for NLP. Existing NLP models,
often trained on general text data, struggle to accurately understand
and process legal queries. For example, identifying the relevant legal

complex [53]. Lawyer-LLaMA [54] is a Chinese legal LLM trained

datasets for supervised fine-tuning, and LexiLaw also mitigates the
catastrophic forgetting problem brought by fine-tuning with general
domain data. Nevertheless, the use of high-quality datasets for pretraining or supervised fine-tuning incurs substantial cost influence.

2.3. Collaborative legal expert prompting
While our work draws on existing research on large language models for law, in-context learning, and legal reasoning, CoLE offers several
new contributions that distinguish it from the state of the art: (I) Unlike
existing prompt engineering or Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)
methods, which often focus on individual techniques, CoLE takes a
holistic, team-based approach. Integrates multiple prompting strategies
and retrieval techniques within a unified framework that mimics the
specialized roles and collaborative workflow of real-world legal teams.
This collaborative framework allows CoLE to synergistically leverage
the strengths of the various techniques, resulting in improved overall
performance. (II) CoLE introduces a novel two-stage filtering mechanism in its Legal Knowledge Retrieval (LKR) and Best Demonstration
Retrieval (BDR) modules. First, a semantic retrieval process identifies
potentially relevant legal articles and precedents. In a second step,
the LLM independently selects the most relevant information from the
candidates retrieved. This LLM-driven selection process overcomes the
limitations of existing methods that rely solely on external selection
strategies, often based on metrics such as similarity or complexity.
CoLE’s approach effectively bridges the gap between the retrieved
instances and the inherent understanding of the LLM, resulting in more
informed and accurate reasoning. This self-selection mechanism ensures that the legal knowledge and demonstrations selected match the
internal representations of the LLM, enabling more effective contextual
learning and reasoning. (III) CoLE’s Prompt Generation (PG) module
incorporates established legal reasoning frameworks, specifically legal syllogisms, and the IRAC (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion)
structure [66]. This structured approach guides the LLM to generate
responses that are not only more accurate but also more transparent
and interpretable. By explicitly defining the legal problem, providing relevant rules, applying those rules to the specific facts of the
case, and formulating a well-reasoned conclusion, CoLE enhances the
ability of LLM to engage in sound legal reasoning. This structured
reasoning process is more similar to the analytical methods used by
human lawyers and increases the trustworthiness and explainability
of the LLM’s results. Together, these innovations contribute to the
superior performance of CoLE in handling complex legal queries, as
demonstrated by our extensive experimental results.

2. Related work
Our work relates to several works in the literature: Large Language Models in Law, In-Context Learning, and Retrieval-Augmented
Generation. We will discuss each of these as follows.
2.1. Large language models in law
The development of Chinese legal large language models primarily revolves around strategies such as training, fine-tuning, and the
construction of knowledge bases to facilitate enhanced information
retrieval from expansive legal datasets [19,32,54,55,59]. These models undergo pre-training on vast repositories of Chinese legal texts,
aiming to strengthen their semantic understanding and applicability in
legal contexts. For example, the Lawyer-LLaMA [54] model integrates
a considerable corpus of legal knowledge during its training phase,
and HanFei [55] utilizes approximately 60 GB of diverse legal data,
including case files, regulatory texts, complaints, and legal news, for
comprehensive parameter-level training. Models such as ChatLaw [18],
LexiLaw [56], Fuzi-Mingcha [59], LAW_GPT [19] and LawLLM [57] are
subjected to supervised fine-tuning using datasets with legal content,
which vary in size from 200,000 to 20 million items. The scarcity of
publicly available law datasets poses a considerable obstacle to conventional fine-tuning methodologies. Models like Lawyer-LLaMA and
ChatLaw aim to augment their performance by incorporating retrieval
modules designed to extract pertinent legal articles when addressing
queries. Such approaches, while beneficial, often require substantial
computational resources and time, presenting significant challenges for
researchers working with legal large language models.
2.2. In-context learning
Large Language Models have demonstrated impressive reasoning capabilities across various tasks, sparking interest in leveraging In-context
learning to enhance task performance [39–41]. In-context learning primarily retrieves relevant input/output pairs for a provided test example
from an annotated dataset. However, performance has been shown
to strongly depend on the selected examples of ICL. Related research
can be broadly divided into two categories: unsupervised methods and
supervised methods [38]. Unsupervised methods utilize the distance of
L2 or the distance of cosine similarity to select examples [60,61]. SelfPrompting proposes to utilize dense semantic embedders to retrieve
relevant examples to improve ICL [62]. Recently, some factors about
demonstration selection have been proposed, such as complexity [45],
diversity [46], difficulty [47], and perplexity [48]. In addition, the

3.2. Query Intent Identification (QII) module
Within the realm of human language habits, a considerable disparity
between written and spoken languages. The former typically leans towards formality, direct content, and explicit meaning, especially in law
problems that often entail professional terminology and concepts [67].
In contrast, oral language often includes numerous mood words or
colloquialisms. We discovered a significant dichotomy between written
and spoken languages during the process of using LLMs to understand
the semantics of legal issues. In scenarios involving spoken languages,
queries tend to contain more irrelevant and disorderly information,
potentially leading to erroneous prediction results outputted by the
legal LLMs. To address this issue, we developed QII which is introduced
in the following.
The QII is tasked with analyzing user queries to identify their potential intent and associated legal domain. In addition, it also functions to
identify relevant legal concepts and terms within the query. It should
be noted that QII does not introduce additional models, but instead
leverages the inherent capabilities of the large language model. Specifically, QII uses the LLMs with prompts to generate multiple keyword
groups representing user query intent, which encompass core issues,
legal terms, and subsequent modules utilize the results generated by
QII for retrieval.

clauses for final reference. The Best Demonstration Retrieval (BDR),
which is similar to the previous researcher, chooses the best case from
previous cases as a reference for the new user question. Moreover,
within the LKR and BDR, there are also small groups of retrieval stages
that cooperate. The Prompt Generation Module (PG) integrates the
content from LKR and BDR to generate the Prompt template of LLM,
and the final decision is made by LLM (highest lawyers). This is the

3.7. Prompt Generation module (PG)
The PG Module constitutes a critical component within the
CoLE framework, responsible for synthesizing the outputs from the
LKR, BDR, and QII modules into a coherent and effective prompt
for the underlying LLM. Drawing inspiration from recent advancements in legal prompting techniques, the PG module has been enhanced to incorporate structured reasoning approaches, specifically legal syllogisms [70] and the IRAC (Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion)
framework [66], to enhance the LLM’s legal reasoning capabilities.
Firstly, utilizing the keywords extracted from the QII module and
the selected legal articles from the LKR module, the PG module identifies the key legal concepts and the specific legal issue at hand. This
information is then structured into a premise-based format, aligning
with the principles of syllogistic reasoning, and explicitly stating the
legal question that needs to be addressed. Secondly, the PG module incorporates the most relevant legal statutes identified by the LKR module
into the prompt. These statutes, representing the applicable legal rules,
provide the LLM with the necessary legal foundation to analyze the
issue. The PG module integrates the selected best demonstration from
the BDR module into the prompt. This demonstration, often containing
a similar legal scenario and its corresponding reasoning, aids the LLM in
applying the retrieved legal rules to the specific facts and context of the
user’s query. Finally, the PG module structures the prompt to explicitly
guide the LLM to synthesize the information presented and formulate
a well-reasoned conclusion, addressing the legal issue presented in the
premise.
By incorporating legal syllogisms and the IRAC structure, the PG
module facilitates a more structured and legally sound reasoning process within the LLM. This structured approach not only enhances
the accuracy and reliability of the LLM’s responses but also makes
its reasoning process more transparent and interpretable, aligning it
more closely with the analytical methods employed by human legal
professionals.

4.3. Evaluation
Setup. The operating system which we use is CentOS Linux release
7.5, and the programming language is Python 3.9.12. Our experiments
are conducted on NVIDIA TESLA A100-40G GPU, the CUDA version
is 12.2, and the deep learning framework is PyTorch with version
2.2.0 and Transformers with 4.38.0. During the inference stage, we set
the following hyperparameters: temperature, top-k, top-p and
repetition_penalty to 0.9, 5, 0.85, and 1.1, respectively.
Metrics. In our experiments, we employ human expert answers
from the test sets as the benchmark for evaluating the quality and
performance of the large language models’ responses to legal questions.
We have defined 3 different metrics in total to measure different types
of tasks:
Accuracy (Acc). For single-choice question datasets (SC) (e.g.,
CAIL2018, CAIL2022, CrimeKgAssitant and JEC_QA), the model’s prediction is considered correct if it exactly matches the answer. If multiple
answers are extracted from the model’s prediction or if no valid answer
matches, we treat it as an error.
𝐹1 -score. For multiple-choice question datasets (MC) (e.g.,
CAIL2019), we use the 𝐹1 -score for evaluation. The 𝐹1 score measures
the harmonic mean of precision and recall.
ROUGE. For Q&A datasets (e.g., LvLin, LawLLM, HanFei, LAW_GPT
and Lawyer-LLaMA), we use the ROUGE score for evaluation. It focuses on the overlap of words between the generated answer and the
reference answer. The ROUGE score is calculated as the average of
ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L scores.
Human Evaluation. In our experiments, we have engaged 10 senior
legal experts to evaluate the answers generated by large language
models. The scoring ranges from 0 to 10 points, requiring the legal
experts to provide manual assessments and annotations based on legal
accuracy, clarity of legal arguments, relevance of cited laws, and other
aspects, along with reasons for their scores.
LLM Auto Evaluation. The prior researches [80,81] demonstrate
that, with appropriate prompt design, the evaluation quality achieved
using LLMs is highly consistent with human judgment. We have incorporated an automated evaluation scoring system based on LLMs.
This system involves presenting legal questions, reference answers,
and generated responses to the large model for scoring in terms of
correctness, effectiveness, objectiveness, clearness, and completeness.

LexGLUE. This stems from the inherent strength of LLMs in semantic
understanding, coupled with CoLE’s ability to provide precise legal

and our framework results in a synergistic enhancement of model
capabilities for processing English legal texts.

CoLE, we conducted ablation studies on two models. We evaluate the

of paramount importance, and its removal (w/o BDR) also leads to
a sharp performance drop. This demonstrates the significant role that
providing more similar answer demonstrations plays in large language
model inference. Third, every component of the proposed CoLE system
plays an indispensable role.

Table 7 presents the ratio of suitable answer case self-selection by
the LLMs across 10 different task datasets. The ranks #1 to #5 shown in
the table represent the order closest to the original user intent, that is,
from the most similar to gradually decreasing similarity. We observe
that the order selected by the LLM varies between different tasks. For
example, in the CAIL2019 (C19) dataset, there is a 71% probability
of selecting the closest distance question (#1), which represents the
group with the closest spatial proximity among #1 to #5 across all
datasets. In contrast, in the CAIL2022 (C22) dataset, there is only
a 5% probability of choosing the closest distance question, but it
possesses the most similar distance within #1 to #5 across all datasets.
In the LvLin dataset, the proportions for each ranking are relatively
consistent, but the difference in the spatial distances between #1 to #5
is twice that of the CAIL2019 dataset. In other datasets, approximately
half of the proportion selects the closest distance question.
This shows that (I) during self-selection, LLMs may consider questions of different similarities as the best demonstration. Similarity is
an essential factor, but not the sole determinant. (II) In the inference
process, the reference demonstration selected by LLMs may involve
more profound logical relationships. Hence, relying solely on the surface similarity of the questions may be insufficient to find the best
demonstration.
Next, we will demonstrate our findings using an example, as shown
in Fig. 6. When the user presents question ‘‘Can the divorce be
handled on the same day?’’ after identification by the intent
module, the retrieval module recalls the 5 most similar questions.
However, LLM ultimately selects question ‘‘Will there still be
a cooling-off period for divorce in 2022?’’ which has
the largest spatial distance among the five questions, as its reference example. We observe that question ‘‘Will there still be a
cooling-off period for divorce in 2022?’’ is semantically
closer to the user’s question, and its answer holds more reference value.
This indicates that after selecting question ‘‘Will there still be
a cooling-off period for divorce in 2022?’’ as a reference,
the answer provided by LLM aligns better with human expectations. Importantly, these findings underscore the need to introduce LLM-based
optimal demonstration selection in our approach.

5.10. Workflow demo of cole
In this section, we use a real legal question to show how CoLE uses
its various modules to create effective prompts for LLMs. The example
question relates to maritime law.
The workflow, shown in Fig. 8, begins with the QII module analyzing the user’s question and extracting important legal terms such
as ‘‘cargo ship’’, ‘‘arrest warrant’’, ‘‘maritime court’’, ‘‘environmental
protection’’ and ‘‘enforcement assistance’’ These extracted keywords
serve as the basis for the subsequent retrieval processes. The LKR module uses these keywords to search the LKE for relevant legal articles.
LKR’s two-stage process, which combines semantic similarity search
with LLM-driven selection, ensures that the retrieved articles are both
semantically related and contextually appropriate for the user’s query.
At the same time, the BDR module searches the DE for relevant previous
cases. Using a similar two-stage approach, BDR retrieves and selects
a case that demonstrates a similar legal issue and factual scenario,
providing valuable context for the LLM. The PG module then integrates
the results of QII, LKR, and BDR into a structured prompt. The template
includes sections for the user’s question, the legal articles retrieved,
the relevant case summary, and specific instructions for the LLM, as
shown in Fig. 8. This structured format ensures that the query is clear
and comprehensive, guiding the LLM to a well-reasoned and legally
sound response. Crucially, this structure encourages the LLM not only
to provide a response but also to justify their reasoning based on the
legal context and precedents provided.

6. Conclusion
In this paper, we explore how to use LLMs to solve those problems existing in Chinese legal. In detail, we propose a novel prompting framework, ‘‘Collaborative Legal Expert’’ (CoLE), taking inspiration from teamwork paradigms in real-world legal case processing.
It presents a practical and efficient solution to the challenges faced
by legal LLMs. The framework’s Query Intent Identification Module
and the two subsequent processes, Legal Knowledge Retrieval and
Best Demonstration Retrieval enhance the LLM’s comprehension of
colloquial user queries and improve the selection of best demonstration
for ICL, respectively. Compared to traditional methods that use neural
networks to train a model from supervising, our method can quickly
obtain a strong model to solve Chinese-oriented legal problems. Our
extensive experiments, conducted across 15 models and 10 datasets,
have yielded compelling evidence that the integration with CoLE’s LLM
substantially improves performance in legal tasks and our proposed
model outperforms all SOTA LLMs in law. Notably, this enhancement
is achieved without the need for annotated datasets or model parameter updates, which not only reduces the financial burden but also
simplifies the implementation process. In the future, we will focus

Limitations and future work
While CoLE demonstrates promising results in enhancing the performance of LLMs in the legal domain, our work has limitations that
provide avenues for future research.
Firstly, our current implementation and experiments primarily focus
on the Chinese legal system. Adapting CoLE to other legal systems
presents significant challenges due to variations in legal frameworks,
languages, and reasoning patterns. Future work will explore crosslingual transfer learning techniques and investigate methods for incorporating diverse legal datasets from different jurisdictions to enhance
CoLE’s cross-cultural applicability. This includes exploring techniques
for aligning legal concepts and terminology across languages and legal
systems.
Secondly, while our evaluation includes both quantitative metrics
and human evaluation by legal experts, expanding the scope of automatic evaluation metrics remains crucial. Current NLP metrics like
ROUGE, while informative, do not fully capture the nuances of legal
reasoning and argumentation. We aim to develop more specialized
evaluation metrics that directly assess the quality of legal arguments,
the accuracy of legal advice, and the ability to predict case outcomes.
