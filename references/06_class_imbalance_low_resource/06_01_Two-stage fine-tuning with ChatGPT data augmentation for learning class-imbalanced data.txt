Classification of long-tailed distributed data is a challenging problem, which suffers from serious class imbalance
and hence poor performance on tail classes, which have only a few samples. Owing to this paucity of samples,
learning on the tail classes is especially challenging for fine-tuning when transferring a pretrained model to a
downstream task. In this work, we present a simple modification of standard fine-tuning to cope with these
challenges. Specifically, we propose a two-stage fine-tuning. In Stage 1, we fine-tune the final layer of the
pretrained model with class-balanced augmented data, generated using ChatGPT. As a large generative language
model, ChatGPT is capable of generating novel and contextually similar responses to a given prompt, which
makes it an excellent candidate for data augmentation. In Stage 2, we perform the standard fine-tuning. Our
modification has several benefits: (1) it leverages pretrained representations by only fine-tuning a small portion
of the model parameters while keeping the rest untouched; (2) it allows the model to learn an initial repre­
sentation of the specific task; and importantly (3) it protects the learning of tail classes from being at a disad­
vantage during the model updating. We conduct extensive experiments on synthetic datasets of both two-class
and multi-class tasks of text classification as well as a real-world application to ADME (i.e., absorption, distri­
bution, metabolism, and excretion) semantic drug labeling. The experimental results show that the proposed
two-stage fine-tuning outperforms vanilla fine-tuning and state-of-the-art methods on the above datasets.

1. Introduction
Real-world classification problems typically exhibit an imbalanced
or long-tailed distribution [1–10], wherein some classes have fewer
samples (minority classes), and some classes have more samples (ma­
jority classes). Such imbalance poses a significant challenge for many
machine learning algorithms [1,4,8,9,11–13]. Poor performance of the
model on the minority classes during the training can have a negative
impact on the overall performance when these classes are represented in
the test set, or if the chosen performance metric places equal importance
on all classes, regardless of their class frequency. The latter has been
done because of various practical concerns, such as fairness or trans­
ferability to new domains [14]. Moreover, the minority classes might
have intrinsic importance, despite being rare [13,15]. Hence, a solution

that improves the performance of the minority classes, while main­
taining the good performance of the majority classes is of crucial
importance.
Fine-tuning is the prevalent paradigm for using large pretrained
language models (LMs) [16–18] to perform downstream tasks. This
paradigm, while widely used, is far from ideal, as the fine-tuning may
predispose the pretrained model to overfitting and generalizing poorly
to out-of-distribution (OOD) data due to the huge model and relatively
small size of the domain-specific data [19–24]. In a class-imbalance
scenario, the model tends to over-fit the minority classes due to their
limited representation, even with the specialized class-balanced tech­
niques such as re-sampling [1,4,13] and re-weighting [14,25–27].

in training, and the impact of the importance weighting diminishes over

2.1. Pretraining and fine-tuning paradigm
Large language models (LMs) have been shown to achieve remark­
able performance across a variety of natural language tasks [16–18],
which ushers in a new era of pretraining and fine-tuning. In this para­

different fine-tuning approaches, vanilla fine-tuning is perhaps the most
popular approach, which fine-tunes some or all the layers of the LM and
then adds one or two simple task-specific output layers (known as pre­
diction heads [36]). In this work, we present a simple modification of
vanilla fine-tuning to cope with imbalanced data.

training the model with balanced augmented data, which allows the

second stage, we simply perform standard or vanilla fine-tuning.
To augment the data for pre-fine-tuning, we exploit ChatGPT to
generate new samples of data that preserve semantic consistency with
existing data samples. ChatGPT is a large language model developed by
OpenAI, based on the GPT-3.5 architecture [30]. ChatGPT has recently
exhibited remarkable capabilities of generating human-like responses to
a wide variety of prompts. Specifically, it is able to capture the context
and meaning of a given sample and generate a new sample that is
consistent with the style, tone, and content. It is the semantic consis­
tency of ChatGPT that enables us to increase the sample size of the
minority classes in the training dataset and thereby enhance the per­
formance of the machine learning models.

2.2. Resampling
It includes either over-sampling the minority classes or undersampling the majority classes, or both. The main shortcoming of oversampling is that it can cause over-fitting due to the repetition of the
samples. In the case of under-sampling, the problem is losing the
important information in the samples that are discarded. Such issues
become even more severe, in a highly imbalanced distribution of the
training data, such as long tail distribution.
2.3. Reweighting
This approach involves adjusting the loss function to compensate for
class imbalance by assigning weights to different samples according to
the class distribution [14,25–27]. There are different importance
weighting schemes. A simple way is to assign sample weights propor­
tionally to the inverse of the class frequency [27,37]. Such a scheme
tends to perform poorly when training on large-scale, imbalanced
datasets [27]. Rather than using the total number of samples present in
each class, reweighting by the effective number of samples is introduced
for better class balance [25]. In addition to adjusting weights to the class
level, re-weighting can also be performed at the sample level by
increasing the weights for hard samples and decreasing the weights for
easy samples [26,38,39]. The downside of assigning higher weights to
hard examples is the overstress on noisy or mislabeled samples [40,41].
Recently, margin approaches such as the label-distribution-aware
margin (LDAM) have been proposed to encourage the minority classes
to have larger margins, leading to a competitive performance on many
benchmark vision tasks [14]. We benchmark our two-stage fine-tuning
against reweighting by the effective number of samples and LDAM.

2.5. Data augmentation
Data Augmentation is a technique used to generate new artificial
samples based on existing samples. This technique has been proven to
increase the model’s generalization capabilities [31,47]; however, the
discrete nature of language data adds another layer of complexity to
implementing data augmentation for NLP [48,49]. Data augmentation
techniques for NLP can be classified into four broad categories, namely,
token-level augmentation, sentence-level Augmentation, adversarial
augmentation, and hidden-space augmentation [50].
Token-level augmentation techniques manipulate tokens or words in
the text to generate a new text, which ideally retains the semantic
meaning of the original text. This can include random insertion, random
swap, random deletion, and synonym replacement [51]. Although such
techniques can achieve interesting results [50], their shortcoming is that
they don’t account for the effect of the context on each word or token
[52]. In sentence-level augmentation, the whole sentence is replaced
with a new sentence. This can be achieved by back translation, i.e.,
translating the sentence to another language and translating back to the
original language [53,54]. Adversarial data augmentation techniques
create new samples by adding adversarial perturbations to the original
text to deceive the victim model [55,56]. Such techniques usually
require expensive computation to determine the direction of the injected
perturbations [57]. Hidden-space data augmentation generates new text
samples by manipulating the hidden representations of the neural
network via operations such as adding noise or interpolating two text
samples [57–59]. In this work, leveraging ChatGPT for data augmen­
tation via rephrasing or rewriting presents a simple, yet effective
approach to generate diverse new samples that maintain semantic
consistency.

3.2. Two-stage fine-tuning
Given a pretrained model (e.g., BERT), in order to maintain a good
overall performance for both majority classes and minority classes, we
propose a two-stage fine-tuning approach to improving imbalanced
classification:
3.2.1. Stage 1: pre-fine-tuning
In this stage, rather than using the original training data, we train the
model with augmented data generated by ChatGPT. The augmented
data is guaranteed to be class-balanced by generating more than one
surrogate sample for each sample in the minority classes. This protects
the minority classes from being at a disadvantage. We call this stage prefine-tuning because it provides a good initial representation for finetuning to the specific downstream task. In order to avoid distorting
the pretrained features, in this stage, we only train the final layer of the
model and the classification head.
Augmented data are obtained by presenting the original data to
ChatGPT. To automate the process of data augmentation, we employ the
OpenAI Python chat completion API, with "gpt-3.5-turbo" as the model
with the default temperature of 1. For each original sample, the input to
the model is instructions such as “Rephrase the following sentence into a
different sentence with a similar meaning", followed by the original text
sample. The model’s response serves as the augmented data. This pro­
cess is applied iteratively until the augmented dataset reaches a
balanced distribution across different classes. Fig. 2 shows an example of
augmented data from the SST-2 train dataset, where one original sen­
tence from a movie review is used to obtain three new sentences of re­
view with similar meanings.
As a comparison, we additionally implement two other methods for
Stage 1. One is back translation, where the augmented samples are ob­
tained using translating the original samples into another language and
then back translating them into English [53,54]. We use German and
Russian, as the intermediate languages for back translation. Half of the
augmented samples were obtained from German, and the other half we
obtained from Russian. Another method used in Stage 1 is reweighting
(RW), where original imbalanced train data is used in pre-fine-tuning,
however, we employ a reweighted loss function, which assigns larger
weights to errors in the minority classes, so that the model fits these
classes, in spite of their limited samples. In this method, we employ
LDAM loss function [14] because of its outstanding performance. The
class weights are calculated using the effective number of samples [25].

3. Methods
3.1. Motivations
Our work was inspired primarily by recent theoretical work and
empirical findings when training deep learning models on imbalanced
datasets. While empirically the class imbalance often leads to inferior
model performance [1,4,13], there remains a lack of theoretical un­
derstanding of its impact on the deep models. A recent theoretical
analysis [29] predicts the emergence of minority collapse in imbalanced
learning, i.e., the minority classes collapse to a single vector in the
topmost layer, which places a fundamental limit on the model perfor­
mance for the minority classes. Further mathematical analysis reveals
that fine-tuning the entire model can distort the pretrained features and
underperform on test examples sampled from OOD distributions [24].
These theoretical studies signify that only the last few layers need to be
fine-tuned when it comes to imbalanced datasets.
The key empirical motivation for our method rests upon recent
experimental work [28] for studying the effects of importance weighting
in deep learning. Across a variety of tasks, architectures, and data sets,
they found that reweighting has a significant effect early in training, and
the impact of importance weighting diminishes over successive epochs
of training. These results provide compelling evidence that the early stage
of training is important to imbalanced learning. Therefore, we are
motivated to add a new learning stage, the pre-fine-tuning, where the

4. Experiments
4.1. General settings
We conducted our experiments on two synthetic benchmarks and
one real-world application. The benchmark datasets were the Stanford
Sentiment Treebank-2 (SST-2) [60] which is a two-class binary senti­
ment classification task, and the 20 Newsgroups [61] which is a
multi-class text classification. We artificially created the different
imbalanced versions of two benchmark datasets by following specific
imbalance distributions, as detailed below.
Furthermore, we applied our approach to a real-world FDA drug
labeling dataset, which aims to enhance the assessment of productspecific guidance (PSGs) [62]. As part of the automation of PSG
assessment, it is desirable to classify pharmacokinetic text paragraphs
from the reference listed drug labeling into different classes, namely,
absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) and other.
We used the ADME dataset, which is naturally imbalanced, to test the
performance of our proposed method.

4.1.1. Implemented methods
For each dataset, we evaluated our proposed method, two-stage finetuning with ChatGPT, as well as the alternative implementations of twostage fine-tuning (back translation and RW). Additionally, we tested
vanilla fine-tuning, where no measure is taken to tackle the imbalanced
data issue, as well as two state-of-the-art methods for learning imbal­
anced data: reweighting by class-balanced (CB) loss function based on
the effective number of samples [25], and reweighting by LDAM loss
function [14].

4.2. Synthetic Benchmark 1: SST-2

4.2.4. Out-of-distribution samples
The OOD samples are the data unseen during training, which usually
have a different distribution than the training data. This is known as the
distribution shift [67], which is a fundamental challenge in machine
learning. As such, the OOD data provides a critical test for the gener­
alization ability of the new method. We, therefore, check this idea by
using the models trained by the SST-2 data and testing it on a different
sentiment analysis dataset: Internet Movie Database (IMDB) reviews
[68]. The IMDB dataset contains 25,000 test samples with an equal
number of positive and negative reviews. Table 4 shows the results of
testing on IMDB data. We observe that overall, our two-stage fine-tuning
with ChatGPT performs better than other methods. This is expected as
the simple modification we made to standard fine-tuning is to only
pre-fine-tune a small portion of the model parameters while keeping the
rest untouched; it hence can preserve pretrained features, which allows
our method to extrapolate better to OOD data.

4.2.1. Dataset
The Stanford Sentiment Treebank [60] is a corpus of sentences from
movie reviews and human annotations of their sentiments, which now is
one of the General Language Understanding Evaluation (GLUE) bench­
mark datasets [64]. In this dataset, each review is labeled either 1 for
positive sentiment or 0 for negative sentiment. There are 37569 positive
reviews and 29780 negative reviews for the train data. The test data is
almost evenly distributed (444 positive and 428 negative).
To create an imbalanced training set, we randomly removed negative
reviews until the number of negative samples was one-fifth of the
number of positive samples. The test dataset remained unchanged.

follows an exponential decay. Specifically, the sample size in the i-th

When reporting the model performance, it has been an ongoing
debate about how to choose an appropriate metric for imbalanced data
[71], particularly for multi-class classification problems. In this work,
we have mainly used the F1-score to assess the model performance,
though other measures can be more appropriate in different domains
such as medical diagnosis. As the F1 score is more sensitive to data
distribution, it is a suitable measure for classification problems on
imbalanced datasets. As we work with both two-class and multi-class
problems, we report both the overall F1 score (macro-F1) and
per-class F1 score to quantify the generalization performance of both
majority and minority classes for the benchmark datasets. We also test
the per-class top-1 error, which obtains similarly consistent perfor­
mance. When reporting the model performance for imbalanced data, we
deem that it is important to explicitly document what evaluation metrics
are exactly used.

not limited to the BERT base.
5. Discussion and conclusion
5.1. Conclusion
Real-world classification problems typically exhibit a long-tailed or
imbalanced distribution, wherein many classes are associated with only
a few samples. This poses a challenge for learning on such data as it is
susceptible to an undesirable bias towards majority classes, hence
leading to poor performance for minority classes. To tackle this issue, we
propose a new learning strategy: two-stage fine-tuning. In Stage 1, we
pre-fine-tune the model with either class-balanced augmented data,
generated using ChatGPT, or a class-balanced reweighing loss, to pro­
vide the minority classes a head-start. In Stage 2 we resume the standard
fine-tuning. We compare our two-stage fine-tuning with vanilla finetuning, LDAM, and CB, and show that our two-stage fine-tuning out­
performs other methods on two manually created imbalanced bench­
mark datasets (both two-class and multi-class) and a real-world
application of ADME semantic labeling. Critically, we show that our
two-stage fine-tuning using ChatGPT possesses a better generalization
ability when tested on OOD data.

5.3. Future work
There are several extensions of our method. First, the effectiveness of
our two-stage fine-tuning is primarily demonstrated in text classification
tasks in this work. It is conceivable that our method can be equally
applied to other domains of machine learning such as computer vision,
where imbalanced data abound. Second, while the method we proposed
in this work is designed for learning imbalanced data, the idea can also
be beneficial for balanced data because the benefits of freezing the initial
layers in the first stage might increase the performance of the balanced
data as well. Third, in the current work, we have limited the fine-tuning
at the first stage to only the final layer, which is mainly determined by
the theoretical analysis [29]. In practice, it is plausible to fine-tune the
last few layers, rather than only the final layer, for improved perfor­
mance. Finally, our work focuses on the imbalanced classification.
Extending our investigation to imbalanced regression [72–74] is also of
interest.

5.2. Discussion
Fine-tuning has become the de facto standard to leverage large pre­
trained models to perform downstream tasks [16–18]. Despite being
widely used, fine-tuning may predispose the pretrained model to over­
fitting and generalizing poorly to OOD data due to the huge model and
relatively small size of the domain-specific data. To address this gap, we
showed that the pre-finetuning we introduced in this work, as an in­
termediate training stage in between the pretrained model and
fine-tuning, is effective to yield better performance. Having such an
additional training stage encourages the pretrained model to be better
adapted to the target data before the vanilla fine-tuning takes place.
ChatGPT has demonstrated remarkable performance in a variety of
intricate linguistic tasks in a zero-shot manner. In this work, we leverage
ChatGPT for data augmentation via rephrasing to generate diverse new
samples that maintain semantic consistency to mitigate the problem of
imbalanced learning. In addition, we implement two additional
methods, RW and back translation, at the first stage of our proposed
strategy. The goal of the first stage is to provide a good initialization for
the standard fine-tuning, i.e., Stage 2, by giving a head start to the mi­
nority classes. The ChatGPT data augmentation method achieves this
goal with class-balanced augmented data, whereas the reweighting is to
assign a larger penalty to the errors in the minority classes in the loss
function of the model. Results of Table 1 demonstrate that our two-stage
fine-tuning using ChatGPT outperforms the state-of-the-art methods of
class-imbalanced training, and other implementations of our proposed
approach.
