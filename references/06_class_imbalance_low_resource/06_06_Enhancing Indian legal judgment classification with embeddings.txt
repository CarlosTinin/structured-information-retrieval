Abstract
Legal document analysis presents significant challenges due to its complexity and
domain-specific nature. This study introduces an innovative approach for classifying
Indian court judgments into legal domains using diverse machine learning and deep
learning techniques. The method incorporates feature engineering and deep learning
algorithms for extracting meaningful features, supported by a wide range of classifiers, including voting classifiers, gradient boosting, and random forest. Embeddings
are generated using models such as InLegalBERT, InCaseLawBERT, CustomInLawBERT, Mamba, T5, RoBERTa, CodeT5, SBERT, DistilBERT, XLM, XLM
Large, LegalBERT, GPT2, ALBERT, Electra, DeBERTa, TFDeBERTa, FlanT5,
FlanT-Large, BART, BigBird Pegasus, LongFormer, and LUKE. To address class
imbalance, the SMOTE technique is employed, and dimensionality is reduced using
PCA and forward feature selection. The T5+SMOTE+feature selection+voting classifier configuration achieves a notable accuracy of 98%, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed approach. These advancements have significant implications
for applications such as document retrieval, legal discovery, and case law analysis,
enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of legal document classification.

1 Introduction
AI has been applied in the legal domain (Sil et al. 2021) since the 1980s (Nitta
and Satoh 2020), initially through expert systems leveraging rules and domain
knowledge for specific tasks, such as product liability dispute resolution (Waterman and Peterson 1981). These early systems were limited in scope and lacked
adaptability for broader applications. With advancements in deep learning, AI’s
role in the legal field has expanded significantly, enabling tasks such as legal
event detection (Yao et al. 2022), legal judgment prediction (Zhong et al. 2020;
Xiao 2018), legal text classification (Chalkidis and Søgaard 2022; Hendrycks
2021), legal information extraction (Hong et al. 2021), and legal content generation (Tonguz et al. 2021). Legal judgment classification, in particular, involves
assigning court decisions to predefined legal domains, such as criminal law, family law, or intellectual property law, using machine learning algorithms trained to
identify patterns within legal texts. This task is crucial for improving information
retrieval, legal trend analysis, and case management. While classification techniques have been effectively applied in countries with standardized legal systems
like the UK, Canada, and the US, the Indian legal system presents unique challenges that remain underexplored (Prabhakar et al. 2022).
The Indian legal system is characterized by its complexity, combining elements of common, civil, and customary law traditions (Jain 2021; Rankin and
Claus 2016). It includes multiple court levels (Supreme Court, High Courts, and
subordinate courts), an extensive body of statutes, and diverse legal principles.
Legal judgments often lack standardized structures and exhibit verbose, domainspecific language, further complicating automated classification (Ruhl and Katz
2015). For instance, judgments may include lengthy narratives before presenting
legal findings, and the terminology varies significantly across legal domains, even
within the same case type.
The scale of India’s judicial system compounds these challenges. With millions of pending cases and thousands of new ones added annually, the Supreme
Court alone faces an immense caseload (Zain et al. 2018; Galanter et al. 2003).
Efficient classification of judgments is critical for managing this workload,
streamlining document retrieval, and supporting case prioritization. However,
most prior research has focused on regions with less complex legal systems, leaving the unique needs of India unaddressed.
To address these gaps, this study proposes a novel method for classifying Indian legal judgments into specific legal domains. The approach combines
machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques with advanced feature
engineering to enhance classification accuracy. Techniques such as SMOTE (synthetic minority over-sampling technique) are utilized to address class imbalance,
ensuring fair representation across legal domains. Additionally, the development
of voting classifier ensembles integrates the strengths of multiple algorithms for
improved performance.

2 An overview of Indian legal system
The Indian legal system is one of the most intricate and historically significant
judicial frameworks in the world. It has evolved as a hybrid system, incorporating
elements of common law, civil law, customary law, and personal laws, shaped by
British colonial influence, ancient Hindu and Islamic legal traditions, and modern
constitutional principles. India follows a federal structure, where both the central
and state governments have the authority to enact laws, with the judiciary playing
a crucial role in interpreting and enforcing them. The Constitution of India serves
as the supreme law, laying down fundamental rights, directive principles, and the
framework for governance. Alongside constitutional law, the legal framework is
shaped by statutory laws enacted by Parliament and state legislatures, judicial
precedents under the doctrine of stare decisis, and customary and personal laws
that govern family matters like marriage, divorce, and inheritance across Hindu,
Muslim, Christian, and Parsi communities.
India’s judicial hierarchy follows a three-tier system. The Supreme Court of
India, as the apex court, has the authority for constitutional review and final
appellate jurisdiction. Below it, High Courts operate at the state level, handling
appeals and writ petitions. Further down, Subordinate Courts, including District Courts, Magistrate Courts, and Special Tribunals, handle civil, criminal,
and administrative disputes. Legal matters in India are categorized into various
domains, such as civil, criminal, constitutional, administrative, family, taxation,
and corporate law. Each domain serves distinct legal purposes—for example,
civil law governs disputes over contracts, property, and rights, while criminal law
deals with offenses like theft and murder. Family law addresses issues related to
marriage, divorce, and child custody, while constitutional law involves the interpretation and enforcement of fundamental rights and governance structures. The
domain and subdoamin are as show in Table 1 with reference that validate the
classification. To modernize India’s legal framework, significant legal reforms
were introduced in 2023, replacing colonial-era laws with updated statutes. The
Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) replaced the Indian Penal Code (IPC), updating criminal definitions and penalties. The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita
(BNSS) replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC), streamlining investigations and trial procedures. The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) replaced
the Indian Evidence Act, expanding the scope of digital and forensic evidence
in legal proceedings. These reforms aim to increase judicial efficiency, improve
access to justice, and modernize procedural laws to better reflect India’s evolving
legal landscape.
In practice, despite recent advancements, professionals and researchers face
significant challenges in managing the vast volume and complexity of Indian
legal texts. Legal judgments often lack a standardized structure, complicating the
extraction of relevant information. Each judgment may be presented in varying
formats, requiring manual parsing and interpretation to identify key elements such
as the case subject, legal arguments, and conclusions. Additionally, the complexity of legal language, with its formalized terms and domain-specific terminology,

3.6 Feature engineering and selection techniques
Zheng et al. (2004) proposed a feature selection approach for text classification in

3.8 Challenges and gaps of exciting technologies
In text classification, an area requiring more attention pertains to the interpretability
of intricate models like deep neural networks. Although these models exhibit remarkable performance across numerous text classification tasks, they often remain enigmatic “black boxes,” posing challenges in comprehending the rationale behind specific
predictions. When juxtaposed with international legal texts, like Chinese, Australian,
and European cases, the spotlight on Indian legal texts has been comparably less pronounced. Further, the prevailing corpus of work primarily revolves around succinct
texts with modestly sized labeled datasets, potentially posing challenges in handling
extended texts and scaling up labeled text classification endeavors within the legal context. The intricacies inherent in legal sources and language usage give rise to challenges
that require developing advanced feature extraction methods and machine learning
models to facilitate the effective processing of legal text.
Studies on legal text classification in the Indian landscape have predominantly
concentrated on analyzing judgments and rulings or have remained confined to specific legal sub-domains like intellectual property or contract law. Consequently, a gap
emerges in developing and evaluating potent machine learning models tailored for
legal text classification in the Indian legal sphere. The proposed methodology seeks
to bridge this gap by integrating diverse deep learning and transformer models recognized for their superiority over conventional methods in NLP tasks. By harnessing
these advanced techniques, the proposed approach strives to achieve heightened accuracy and efficiency in classifying legal documents, thereby surmounting the limitations
posed by existing methodologies.

4 Dataset
The dataset consists of 3229 legal judgments carefully curated to represent the
diverse and complex nature of the Indian legal system. Sourced from IndianKanoon.
com, a publicly accessible and widely recognized legal database, these judgments
encompass decisions from the Hon’ble Supreme Court of India (SC) and various
High Courts (HCs), spanning the period from 1950 to 2022. This extensive temporal coverage ensures the inclusion of both historical and contemporary judgments,
reflecting the evolution of legal reasoning and practices in India. All judgments are
available in English, making them suitable for academic research without licensing
restrictions. The dataset spans 14 distinct legal domains, including Civil, Criminal,
Writ, Income Tax, Constitution, Land Revenue, Family, Execution Petition, Sales
Tax, Revision Petition, Juvenile Justice, Labour Law, Excise, and Disciplinary Jurisdiction. These domains capture the hybrid nature of India’s legal framework, which
blends Common Law, Civil Law, and customary traditions. For example, Criminal
Law and Constitutional Law are rooted in common law principles, while Family
Law incorporates religious personal laws alongside statutory frameworks. Domains
such as Taxation, governed by detailed statutory codes, and Labour Law, which integrates statutory provisions with judicial precedents, further illustrate the diversity
of the system. This comprehensive coverage ensures the dataset reflects the varying
nature of legal language and reasoning across different contexts.
The dataset was constructed using a two-stage sampling methodology. Initially, a
pool of approximately 5000 judgments was collected from IndianKanoon based on
criteria such as precedential value, structural completeness, and clarity of legal reasoning. From this pool, a systematically sampled set of 3229 judgments was selected
to align with the official domain distribution patterns reported by the Supreme Court
of India. This approach ensured that the dataset mirrored the relative proportions
of cases across domains, such as 23.5% Civil and 19% Criminal, as seen in official judicial statistics. Priority was given to judgments with well-articulated legal

5 Methodology
The objective of legal domain classification is to classify legal records into various
domains, such as civil, criminal, family law etc. Accurate classification of legal data
can assist legal professionals in efficiently processing and managing large volumes
of legal documents. In this paper, a novel methodology for legal domain classification that leverages machine learning techniques and feature selection methods is
proposed as shown in Fig. 7. The proposed methodology consists of four main steps:
data preprocessing, feature extraction, Feature selection and classification.
5.1 Preprocessing
The dataset consisting of legal judgments from different domains is collected from
relevant sources as explained in Section III. The dataset does not include any document metadata, which is a common feature in structured legal datasets. In this
case, the judgments were manually collected, and the required classifications (e.g.,

Fig. 7  Illustration of the proposed method for legal document domain classification

domain categorization) were created after thoroughly reading each document. This
step ensured that the documents were categorized based on their legal domain. The
gathered data is subjected to preprocessing, which involves eliminating any noise
and irrelevant information while transforming the text into a suitable format for
analysis. This stage incorporates various techniques, including tokenization, removal

P. Prabhakar, P. B. Pati

of stop words, stemming, and lemmatization, to accomplish the task. Text Cleaning involves removing any irrelevant or unnecessary text such as special characters,
punctuations, and numbers from the dataset. Tokenization step involves breaking down the text into separate words or tokens. Following tokenization, stopword
removal encompasses the elimination of commonly used words, such as “a,” “an,”
“the,” and similar terms, which carry minimal significance in the text. Lemmatization involves reducing words to their base or root form, so that variations of the
same word are treated as the same token. Part-of-Speech tagging involves identifying grammatical category, such as noun, adjective, verb, and so on, of each token in
the text.
5.2 Data augmentation
The SMOTE is a widely used approach in resolving data imbalance problems, specifically in classification tasks. It involves oversampling the minority class by generating artificial instances via interpolation between adjacent instances of the minority
class, aiming to address the imbalance issue and prevent the classifier from favoring
the majority class. The SMOTE algorithm selects k closest minority class instances
and generates synthetic instances by interpolating between them in feature space,
which results in more representative and robust synthetic instances. The sampling
ratio, a user-defined parameter, controls the number of synthetic instances for each
minority class instance. SMOTE has demonstrated its effectiveness in enhancing the
accuracy of classifiers for imbalanced data, including fraud detection, medical diagnosis, and credit scoring. However, it is worth mentioning that SMOTE may not be
effective in scenarios where the class imbalance is severe or the feature distributions
of the minority and majority classes are significantly different. Moreover, the selection of the k parameter plays a crucial role in the SMOTE algorithm’s effectiveness,
and it might need some trial and error to determine the optimal value. Furthermore,
the legal domain can benefit from employing data augmentation approaches, including the synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE), to handle imbalanced
class distributions. By generating synthetic instances of the minority class, SMOTE
can rebalance the class distribution and enhance the classifiers’ performance.

5.4 Feature selection
Filter methods in feature selection involve selecting the most important features
based on some statistical measure or evaluation metric, without involving the
machine learning model. The forward feature selection method (Bharti and Singh
2014) is used, which involves adding one feature at a time and selecting the subset
that yields the best model performance. By iteratively adding one feature at a time
and selecting the subset that resulted in the best model performance, the most relevant features were identified. The feature selection aimed at improving the performance of the learning model by identifying the most informative features and reducing the redundancy in the feature set.

Classification of legal domain is a significant problem in the area of NLP and judicial system. The legal domain consists of multiple sub-domains such as civil, criminal, family, constitution etc. which can be classified using different AI techniques.

legal domain classification task. The combination of transformer models and traditional machine learning models or deep learning models is expected to provide

5.6 Evaluation
In this study, the evaluation of the deep learning models was performed using two
important metrics: Mean Accuracy and Standard Deviation. A fivefold cross-validation approach was chosen for evaluation. This technique strikes a balance between
computational efficiency and reliable performance estimation. Mean Accuracy was
calculated by averaging the accuracy scores obtained from each fold, providing an
overall measure of the classifier’s performance and mitigating potential variations
across folds. Additionally, Standard Deviation was used to quantify the variability
of the classifier’s performance across different folds, indicating the consistency of its
performance. A smaller standard deviation suggests more consistent performance,
while a larger standard deviation may indicate greater variability. The chosen evaluation metrics, provide valuable insights into the models’ performance, generalization ability, and consistency.

6.2 Discussion
The experiments conducted in this study demonstrated the utility of specific embedding techniques—such as GloVe, FastText, and T5—when combined with deep learning models like BiLSTM for the classification of Indian legal judgments. These experiments emphasized the importance of selecting appropriate embeddings, handling class
imbalance, and incorporating feature selection techniques for optimal performance.
Class imbalance handling through techniques like SMOTE showed mixed results.
SMOTE, which generates synthetic samples for the minority class, was found to
reduce accuracy when used with deep learning models. This may be due to the efficient handling of class imbalance by deep learning models themselves, with the
introduction of synthetic samples potentially leading to overfitting, especially in
smaller legal domains. However, combining SMOTE with feature selection yielded
better results overall, as it mitigated the negative effects of class imbalance. The corresponding accuracy values for experiments involving SMOTE and feature selection
with ML classifiers are provided in Appendix.
Transformer-based models like T5 outperformed other models due to their ability
to capture complex relationships through self-attention mechanisms, allowing better
understanding of legal text’s semantic nuances. Despite this, domain-specific models like InLegalBERT, IncaselawBERT, and CustomInLawBERT showed a marginal
decrease in performance (2–5% lower) compared to T5-based models. This could be
attributed to the complexity and diversity of the legal domain, which might not be
fully captured by these models. Additionally, T5’s pretraining (Zheng et al. 2021)
and generalization capabilities made it more suitable for the task despite not being
fine-tuned on Indian legal datasets (Prabhakar et al. 2022).

546

Some models, particularly those using basic embeddings like FastText, showed
relatively low performance (below 60% accuracy), even when the dataset size was
increased from 900 to 3229 cases. This marginal improvement suggests that the
complexity and diversity of the legal domain were underrepresented, limiting the
models’ ability to capture nuanced patterns. Moreover, the intricacies of legal language and variability across domains contributed to lower accuracy with simpler
embeddings. Therefore, improvements in dataset size, diversity, and more advanced
modeling techniques are needed for significant performance gains.
The experiments were conducted using Google Colab with Python 3, utilizing the
Google Compute Engine backend. The system resources included 12.7 GB of RAM
(1.8 GB used) and 107.7 GB of disk space (33.1 GB utilized), which were sufficient
for executing the models. However, hardware limitations may have influenced the
overall runtime and efficiency. In conclusion, the combination of transformer-based
embeddings, feature selection, and ensemble methods proved to be the most effective strategy for achieving high classification accuracy, with the best performance
reaching 0.99. However, further research into domain-specific models, error analysis, and class-wise performance is needed to improve model robustness and explainability for legal document classification. The scalability of transformer embeddings
combined with ML classifiers offers a cost-effective solution for automating legal
document summarization in resource-constrained settings, making it a promising
approach for large-scale legal applications requiring real-time processing.

7 Conclusion
The legal domain classification is a crucial task that plays a vital role in many applications such as legal document summarization, legal recommendation systems, and
legal decision making. In this paper, we proposed a methodology for legal domain
classification using deep learning and machine learning models. We performed feature extraction using transformer models and applied oversampling techniques such
as SMOTE to handle class imbalance. We also evaluated the performance of various
models such as Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, other machine learning
classifiers Transformer models, CNN, RNN and LSTM on the proposed dataset. The
experimental results showed that the transformer-based feature extraction combined
with the ML models outperformed the other models, achieving an accuracy of 99%.
In the future, there is a need for further research to improve the performance
of legal domain classification. One possible direction for future research is to
explore the use of more advanced transformer models such as GPT-3 for feature
extraction. It would be interesting to explore the use of other feature extraction
techniques on the performance of these models and also fine-tuning the transformers could potentially improve performance. Additionally, it would be valuable to collect larger and more diverse legal datasets to further evaluate the
generalizability of these models in real-world legal applications. Additionally,
investigating the use of ensemble learning techniques and other oversampling
methods such as ADASYN and SMOTE-NC can help improve the classification accuracy. Another future research direction is to evaluate the proposed
methodology on larger and more diverse legal datasets from different jurisdictions to test its generalizability. Furthermore, the proposed methodology can
be extended to other applications such as legal question answering systems and
legal case prediction. Overall, legal domain classification is an exciting area of
