Multi-label document classification has a broad range of applicability to various practical problems,
such as news article topic tagging, sentiment analysis, medical code classification, etc. A variety of
approaches (e.g., tree-based methods, neural networks and deep learning systems that are specifically
based on pre-trained language models) have been developed for multi-label document classification
problems and have achieved satisfying performance on different datasets. In the legal domain, however,
one is often faced with several key challenges when working with multi-label classification tasks. One
critical challenge is the lack of high-quality human labeled datasets, which prevents researchers and
practitioners from achieving decent performance on respective tasks. Also, existing methods on multilabel classification typically focus on the majority classes, which results in an unsatisfying performance
for other important classes that do not have sufficient training samples. In order to tackle the above
challenges, in this paper, we first present POSTURE50K, a novel legal extreme multi-label classification
dataset, which we will release to the research community. The dataset contains 50,000 legal opinions
and their manually labeled legal procedural postures. Labels in this dataset follow a Zipfian distribution,
leaving many of the classes with only a few samples. Furthermore, we propose a deep learning
architecture that adopts domain-specific pre-training and a label-attention mechanism for multi-label
document classification. We evaluate our proposed architecture on POSTURE50K and another legal
multi-label dataset EUROLEX57K, and show that our approach achieves better performances than two
baseline systems and another four recent state-of-the-art methods on both datasets.

1. Introduction
Multi-label document classification has a broad range of realworld applications, such as sentiment analysis [1], medical code
classification [2], tweets classification [3], etc. A variety of methods have been developed for such problems, including traditional
one-vs-all classifiers [4,5], classical machine learning approaches
(e.g., Random Forest [6] and Multi-layer Perceptron [7]) and deep
neural networks [8–10]. Advancements in machine learning and
related fields have enabled multi-label classification algorithms
to achieve continued improvements on datasets from different
domains [11–16].
In the legal domain, there exists a strong demand for high performing multi-label classification algorithms for different tasks,
such as legal motion detection [17] and case outcome prediction [18]. However, researchers and practitioners are often faced
with two major challenges. On one hand, there exists only a few

2.2. Multi-label datasets
Several multi-label/multi-class datasets already exist, such as
the Reuters Corpus Volume I (RCV1) [11], the Amazon review
data [12], Wikipedia Tags [14], MIMIC-III [13], EURLEX57K [16],
ECHR [30] and CAIL2018 [15]. These datasets cover a variety
of domains, including news [11], medicine [13], online platform
(e.g., Wikipedia and Amazon) [12,14] and legal [15,30]. In terms of
size, they vary substantially, ranging from 20K tagged Wikipedia
articles [14] to millions of online product reviews [12] or legal
cases [15].
The most similar datasets to POSTURE50K are ECHR [30] and
CAIL2018 [15]. The European Court of Human Rights (ECHR) hears
allegations that a state has breached human rights provisions of
the European Convention of Human Rights. The task is to predict
whether any human right protocol has been violated (binary classification) and if so, which protocols (multi-label classification).
It contains 11.5K cases from ECHR’s public database1 . Compared
to ECHR, our dataset is much larger with 50K legal cases and
covers diverse legal areas. CAIL2018 contains 2.6 million cases
from China’s Supreme Court and includes three tasks: finding the
law articles that were violated, determining the charges (e.g., intentional injury) and prison term (e.g., six months of prison time).
Although this dataset is at a much larger scale than ours, it
only contains criminal cases while our dataset covers different
legal areas, such as civil, criminal, bankruptcy, etc. Furthermore,
in CAIL2018, for the tasks of predicting violated law articles
and determining charges, there are 183 and 202 different categories respectively while our dataset has 256 different types of
procedural postures.

3. The POSTURE50K dataset

Dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction is not a final
judgment from which an appeal will lie. Schwenker v. St.
Louis County National Bank, 682 S.W.2d 868, 870
(Mo.App.1984). The right of an appeal is statutory, and an
appeal may be taken only from a final judgment. In this
connection, a dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction is
by the terms of the statute § 510.150, RSMo 1986 and
Rule 67.03, without prejudice unless the order of dismissal

extraordinary writ but not by appeal.

POSTURE50K2 includes 50K legal opinions from the United

3.1. Background
Procedural Postures are motions that a judge has previously
ruled on and are subsequently challenged in a higher court. In
addition, they also need to be substantively discussed by the
judge of the higher court. They range from very common motions (e.g., Motion to Dismiss) to rare types (such as Motion to
Admonish Jury). Postures may seem to be easy to identify by
simply matching keywords in a legal case. For example, Case
13 below has a procedural posture of Motion to Dismiss for Lack
of Personal Jurisdiction as clearly indicated by the bolded text.
The entire context, however, is important and only if the motion
is sufficiently discussed by the judge a procedural posture is
warranted.

In the second example, we can see that there are sometimes legal proceedings and reasoning discussed in the opinions
that would require a more sophisticated approach to detecting
procedural postures beyond the use of simple keywords. Our
experiments (Section 5) show that a Random Forest (RF) [6]
classifier using n-grams achieved a Micro-F1 score of 0.755 but a
Macro-F1 score of only 0.053. This indicates that many postures
can indeed be identified by specific keywords while actual understanding of legal procedures and reasoning in the case texts
would be required in order to achieve better coverage for the
broad range of classes (i.e., higher Macro-F1 scores).

3.2. Expert annotation
We hired a team of editors to manually tag cases with procedural postures. All editors have professional legal background and
were further trained to perform this specific tagging task. Each
case may be tagged with one or more postures, thus making this
a multi-label dataset. In total, the dataset includes 256 different
posture categories6 . The labels may be hierarchical (e.g., Motion to
Dismiss and its sub-categories), and the editors were instructed to
tag with the most specific type. On average, each case was tagged
with 1.54 postures, with the maximum and minimum being 9 and
0, respectively7 .
During tagging, due to limited resources, each case was worked
on by a single editor. To further examine the reliability of the
groundtruth, we performed an additional inter-annotator study.
Based upon the tagged procedural postures, we randomly selected 200 cases that contain the ten most frequent posture categories and another 200 cases that contain the 80 least frequent
posture categories. We then invited four legal professionals8 to
tag the two groups of 200 cases independently, i.e., two of them
were asked to tag the first group of 200 cases with the top
ten posture categories and the other two annotators were asked
to tag the other 200 cases with the 80 least frequent posture
categories. This way, our inter-annotator study examines both the
popular and the rare posture categories.

Groundtruth Analysis. We present an analysis on the tagged
procedural postures in Fig. 1. We can see that among the 256
posture categories, only eight of them (the very right bar) occur

Content Analysis. In addition to its multi-label and few-shot/
zero-shot nature, there are also a few additional challenges. First
of all, adopting existing off-the-shelf NLP tools to process the data
may be challenging, e.g., PoS tagging, sentence splitting, etc. Many
of the available tools were only built with/for standard English
texts (e.g., news); thus, they may not work well for processing
legal texts. Furthermore, many of the case documents in this
dataset are (extremely) long. In Table 2, we see that the average
number of paragraphs is 30 and the average number of words is
2,901. As further demonstrated in Fig. 2, the majority of the cases
have more than 500 words. For the training set (Train), counting
the two bars from right, there are 11,156 cases with more than
3,000 words, accounting for 35% of the Train split. Therefore, this
dataset presents significant challenges for some of the state-ofthe-art techniques (e.g., BERT [26] and other Transformer [34]
based approaches) due to their difficulties in handling long texts.
In this paper, our proposed system (Section 4) focuses on how
to improve classification coverage, especially on low-frequency
classes; however, it would be interesting to study how well some
of the recent efforts (e.g., Reformer [35] and Longformer [36])
could handle this data from the long-text perspective in future
work.

3.3. Dataset splits
In order to make this dataset suitable for supervised learning
tasks, we split it into Train, Development (Dev) and Test sets. Table 2 shows the high-level statistics of the resulting POSTURE50K
dataset. Instead of performing a simple random split, we adopted
certain rules in the process in order to make this dataset more
challenging and also suitable for different tasks. We split the
dataset as follows:

• Step 1: At the very beginning, we randomly divide all the
cases into three splits, aiming for the ratios of 0.64 (Train),
0.16 (Dev), and 0.20 (Test).
• Step 2: In this dataset, several posture categories only occur
in a single legal case. Therefore, we move all cases that are
tagged with such posture categories to Test, making this
dataset a good candidate for evaluating zero-shot learning
techniques.
• Step 3: Furthermore, because certain posture categories
were only tagged a handful of times (i.e., they occur in
two or just a few cases), our random split may put all
cases with such postures into Train, which makes those
posture categories useless. Therefore, we check whether any
posture category occurs in Train only, Dev only, Train and
Dev only, or Dev and Test only. If so, for each of such posture
categories, we evenly re-distribute its cases into the three
splits by prioritizing Test over Train and Train over Dev. For
example, when a posture category only occurs in two cases,
we put one in Test and another one in Train.
• Step 4: In Step 3, it is possible that a case occurs in more
than one of the splits. For instance, when re-distributing
two different posture categories one after another, a case
that was tagged with both postures may be re-distributed
twice into different splits. Thus, as a last step, we check
the disjointness of the three splits and remove cases that
occur in more than one split from less important splits

As shown in Fig. 4, the RoBERTa architecture is the exact
same architecture as BERT. It consists of an embedding layer, the
RoBERTa transformer, and a language model head. More specifically, the embedding layer (Fig. 5) consists of three different
embedding types. First, the token key embedding maps a one-hot
representation of a token to a dense representation. There is also
a position embedding which maps a token’s absolute position to
a dense representation, so the transformer architecture can learn
the spatial correlation of the words within a passage of text. The
final embedding layer maps a token’s sequence membership to a
feature vector, which has utility when the input passage consists
of several spans of text. Some examples include masking words
in a pair of sentences, sentence pair classification, etc. The output
of each of these layers is elementwise summed and fed into the
transformer block.

4. Label-attended multi-label classification with domainspecific pre-training
In this section, we formally present our deep-learning based
approach for multi-label classification. Generally speaking, our
approach consists of three major components as demonstrated
in Fig. 3: Domain-specific Pre-training, Label-attention and Multitask learning. We will introduce the details of each component in
the rest of this section.
4.1. Domain-specific language model pre-training
OOB language models (such as BERT [36] and RoBERTa [19])
were designed for language understanding before applying to
downstream tasks, such as question answering, text classification,
etc. The task of fitting these models to the English language,

4.2. Label-attended multi-task learning
As shown in Fig. 1, 186 classes in the POSTURE50K dataset
have 50 or fewer cases, which presents a significant challenge for
statistical machine learning systems to produce decent models.
In order to address this issue, we propose label-attended multitask learning where we utilize the embeddings of the class labels
(e.g., the embeddings of Motion to Dismiss, On Appeal, etc.) to
bridge the semantic gap between samples and the classes.
In Fig. 3, the green rectangles represent the components of the
label-attention mechanism and the texts in parentheses indicate
the shape of the corresponding matrices. To start, we have the
Label Embedding where we represent each class label as a vector (of dimension Dim). Then, we compute the cosine distances
between such label embeddings and the output of the domainspecific RoBERTa model, and use the distances as a weight matrix
(Weight Matrix All). The intuition behind this weight matrix is
that we utilize it to measure how similar the RoBERTa output
is to the class labels in high-dimensional space in order for the
learning process to better capture the low-frequency classes. In
the meantime, the output of the domain-specific RoBERTa model
is also sent to a dense layer (Dense_All) in order to classify a
sample to the label space (Dense Output). Finally, instead of using
this dense output as the final results, we multiply it with the
weight matrix (i.e., Weight Matrix All, the cosine distance matrix)
in order to produce the final classification output. Essentially,
the label embedding and the cosine weight matrix work as an
attention mechanism to the model output, projecting it closer to
the labels.
There are different ways to initialize the label embeddings. The
simplest approach is to adopt randomized initialization, which
may not work well given the complexity of the RoBERTa architecture. Another option is to use the average of the embeddings
of the words in the labels; however, with this option, we lose
the connections between the words in the labels by treating
them (mostly) as independent. In this work, we initialize the
label embeddings by using Sentence Transformers [38], a sentence embedding model fine-tuned on top of the out-of-the-box
RoBERTa Large model. By treating class labels as ‘‘sentences", we
produce their sentence embeddings and use them as the initial
weights of Label Embedding. During training, we make the label
embeddings trainable, so that they continue to be adjusted for
the given dataset and the specific classification task.
In order to further strengthen the signals of the low-frequency
classes, we also adopt the idea of multi-task learning where
the second task is to classify only the small classes (determined
by how many training samples a class has in the dataset). The
architecture of this second task (the yellow rectangles in Fig. 3)
is the same as the primary task with two differences. First of all,
the label embedding for this second task only consists those of
the small classes. Also, the dense layer (Dense_Small) projects the
RoBERTa output only to the label space of the small classes. In
our system, we use the multiplied output (Final Output in Fig. 3)
as the final results while another alternative is to use ensemble
on the outputs of the two tasks (e.g., by averaging the outputs of
the small classes from both tasks); however, we found that it did
not help during our evaluation.

5.1. Evaluation setup
Datasets. We evaluate our system on two datasets: POSTURE50K (Section 3) and EUROLEX57K [16]. EUROLEX57 is another legal multi-label dataset that covers a variety of topics,
such as environment, finance, transportation, etc. It contains
three splits: Train, Dev and Test with 45K, 6K and 6K documents
respectively. The task is to classify each document to a predefined set of topics where each document may be assigned more
than one topic, i.e., multi-label classification. In total, there are
4,271 different classes. EUROLEX57 is a newer and larger version
of the well-adopted EUROLEX dataset [39], thus we selected it for
our evaluation.
Metrics. State-of-the-art systems often utilize the Precision at
k (P@k) scores as the evaluation metrics [8–10]; however, later in
this section, we show that these metrics may not truly reflect the
capabilities of multi-label classification systems. In this paper, we
adopt the Micro-F1 score, the Macro-F1 score and the Weighted
Macro-F1 score (including their corresponding precision and recall) as our main evaluation metrics. In addition, we also examine
and discuss the training time for different types of models, shedding some light on the trade-offs between performance gains and
(time and monetary) cost.
Comparison Systems. First of all, we use Random Forest
(RF ) [6] and Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP) [7] as two baselines
where we only use simple n-gram features. Furthermore, we
compare to two attention-based state-of-the-art neural networks
that do not use pre-trained language models: BIGRU-LWAN [16]
and AttentionXML [8]. Finally, we evaluate our proposed deep
learning architecture Label-attended Multi-task Multi-label
Classification (LAMT_MLC ) against another two state-of-the-art
systems that are also based on pre-trained language models:
APLC_XLNet [9] and X-Transformer [10]. For APLC_XLNet, the original implementation uses XLNet Base while our proposed system
uses RoBERTa Large; thus, we changed its implementation to
use XLNet Large in order to perform a fair comparison. For
X-Transformer, we use its RoBERTa Large option.
Parameter Tuning. We show the tuned parameters in Table 3
for RF and MLP. For MLP, we train it for 50 and 100 iterations
on POSTURE50K and EUROLEX57K respectively and obtain its
performance scores. For both systems, we tuned the optimal
number of n-gram features ranging from 1K to 6K features.
For BIGRU-LWAN [16], we experimented with its major parameters: number of layers (1 to 3), size of hidden states (300,
600 and 900), learning rate (1e-3, 1e-4, 1e-5, 2e-5, 3e-5 and 5e5), dropout rate (0.1 to 0.4) and batch size (8, 16 and 32). For
AttentionXML [8], we tuned the important parameters, including
size of hidden states (64, 128, 256, 512 and 1,024), number of
layers (1 and 2) and dropout rate (0.4, 0.5, 0.6 and 0.7). We did not
adopt its ensemble option because it needs to build three trees,
sum the scores, and obtain the final results by considering the
top-k ranked predictions for each sample. This makes it difficult
to compute the F1 scores via a thresholding strategy on the
probabilities.
For APLC_XLNet [9], we focused on tuning the three learning rates that it uses for different modules (XLNet layers, the
sequence summary layer and the final dense layer), and only

Precision at k scores (P@k where k normally equals to 1, 3 and
5) [8–10]. However, during our evaluation, we found that P@k
may not be the most appropriate metric. Fig. 7 shows the P@1 and

Monetary and Time Cost Analysis. A major goal of research
is to keep improving the scores; however, when adopting these
techniques in a real-world setting, we unavoidably need to consider the trade-offs between better scores and the (time and
monetary) cost to produce those models. For example, on the
POSTURE50K dataset, AttentionXML and LAMT_MLC needed about
2 and 30 minutes respectively to train one epoch. In total, the two
systems took about 1 and 15 hours respectively for 30 epochs of
training. Given that the EC2 instance we are using costs about
4 USD/hour, this translates to a monetary cost of 4 USD for
AttentionXML and 60 USD for LAMT_MLC. These additional time
and monetary costs gave us about 1% better Micro-F1 score and
6% higher Macro-F1 score. For a real-world application where
the goal is to simply focus on the majority classes, AttentionXML
is clearly a better choice; however, when trying to be more
comprehensive on detecting the small classes, we would choose
LAMT_MLC. Depending on the application scenarios, one may
choose the more appropriate approaches. In future work, one
interesting research direction is to explore strategies to speed up
the training of those complex models, so that we could enjoy their
better performance with a more acceptable (time and monetary)
cost [40–42].
Datasheet and Ethical Considerations. We will release the
POSTURE50K dataset to the research community for free. Editors
involved in this study were compensated according to their skills,
and the intended use of this dataset is for research purposes only.
With the release, we will also provide a datasheet [43], releasing more specific information on how the data was collected,
the intended use and the license, and how the dataset will be
maintained in more detail.
We do not anticipate the labeled dataset to contain biases
or lead to unwanted societal harm, and prohibit using the data
for unethical purposes, such as bail/sentencing/crime prediction,
which have been shown to be harmful for minority groups.

In this paper, we first describe the POSTURE50K dataset that
we will release to the research community, including the manual
tagging process, the details of the groundtruth and the textual
content. This dataset presents a real-world and challenging multilabel classification task, i.e., tagging legal cases with procedural
postures, a very important concept in the legal domain and for
the litigation process. Furthermore, we propose LAMT_MLC, a
deep learning system that is based on the RoBERTa language
model for performing multi-label classification. Instead of using
the OOB RoBERTa model, we performed further pre-training on
domain-specific datasets. When targeting datasets with a long
tail of labels where some labels lack sufficient training samples,
we adopted a label-attention mechanism and multi-task learning
in order to achieve better coverage, especially on small classes.
By carrying out an evaluation with two large-scale datasets, our
proposed system substantially outperforms two baseline and another four state-of-the-art systems. For future work, it would be
interesting to continue exploring how to better handle classes
without sufficient training samples (e.g. by using data augmentation techniques [44,45]). It would also be interesting and necessary to explore how to better deal with the long texts both
algorithmically and computationally.
