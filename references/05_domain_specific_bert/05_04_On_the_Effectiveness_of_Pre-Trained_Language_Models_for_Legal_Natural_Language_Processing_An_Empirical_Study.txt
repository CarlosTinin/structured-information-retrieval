Processing: An Empirical Study

ABSTRACT We present the first comprehensive empirical evaluation of pre-trained language models (PLMs) for legal natural language processing (NLP) in order to examine their effectiveness in this domain.
Our study covers eight representative and challenging legal datasets, ranging from 900 to 57K samples,
across five NLP tasks: binary classification, multi-label classification, multiple choice question answering,
summarization and information retrieval. We first run unsupervised, classical machine learning and/or nonPLM based deep learning methods on these datasets, and show that baseline systems’ performance can
be 4%∼35% lower than that of PLM-based methods. Next, we compare general-domain PLMs and those
specifically pre-trained for the legal domain, and find that domain-specific PLMs demonstrate 1%∼5%
higher performance than general-domain models, but only when the datasets are extremely close to the pretraining corpora. Finally, we evaluate six general-domain state-of-the-art systems, and show that they have
limited generalizability to legal data, with performance gains from 0.1% to 1.2% over other PLM-based
methods. Our experiments suggest that both general-domain and domain-specific PLM-based methods
generally achieve better results than simpler methods on most tasks, with the exception of the retrieval
task, where the best-performing baseline outperformed all PLM-based methods by at least 5%. Our findings
can help legal NLP practitioners choose the appropriate methods for different tasks, and also shed light on
potential future directions for legal NLP research.

I. INTRODUCTION

In recent years, pre-trained language model (PLM) based
methods have gained significant traction in the natural
language processing (NLP) field among both academic
researchers and industrial practitioners. They typically follow
a ‘‘pre-train and fine-tune’’ paradigm. First, a PLM is
pre-trained on large-scale corpora of texts on masked word
prediction, next sentence prediction, and/or other tasks in
order to learn dependencies and contextual information over

These include SCIBERT [6], BioBERT [7], MatBERT [8],

NLP tasks in the legal domain present several unique and
significant challenges. First of all, the lack of large-scale,
high-quality datasets has been a major hurdle in advancing
legal NLP research. More so than other domains, annotating
legal documents for a specific task requires substantial
domain knowledge, which makes annotation processes both
time-consuming and monetarily expensive. Furthermore,
legal documents possess a distinctive vocabulary, containing
both unique legal terms (e.g., ‘‘estoppel’’) as well as terms
with specific legal meanings that differ from their regular
English meanings. For instance, the word ‘‘standing’’ in
the legal sense refers to a party’s right to initiate a lawsuit
or petition; however, in general-domain texts, ‘‘standing’’
typically encodes the meaning of the verb ‘‘to stand’’.
Also, legal texts may require custom pre-processing, which
poses a challenge for existing NLP tools developed for
general-domain texts. For instance, legal citations (e.g.,
Schwenker v. St. Louis County National Bank, 682 S.W.2d
868, 870 (Mo.App.1984)) in caselaw opinions are extremely
important as they may indicate the precedents1 for new
cases, and methods have been specifically developed for the
extraction and resolution of such information [11]. Lastly,
legal documents are often significantly longer than texts from
other domains. For example, Song et al. showed that in a
dataset of 50K U.S. cases, about 35% of them have 3,000
words or more [12]. In contrast, news stories typically consist
of only a few hundred words. This poses another critical
challenge for legal NLP systems, especially those based on
PLMs.
Despite the challenges mentioned above, PLMs have
drawn substantial attention from legal NLP researchers.
A growing number of datasets have been released to facilitate
research in this area on different tasks, including binary
classification [13], [14], multi-label classification [12], [15],
[16], question answering [14], [17], summarization [18]–[20]
and information retrieval [21]. Datasets in languages other
than English have also become more readily available, such
as a trilingual (French/German/Italian) outcome prediction
dataset [22], the 2.6 million-case dataset from China’s
Supreme Court [23], and GerDaLIR (a German information
retrieval dataset with 123K queries) [24].
Much effort has been devoted to the design and
development of novel techniques for legal NLP applications. Vacek et al. [25] implemented an outcome extraction system that combines SVM [26] and deep neural
networks. Chalkidis et al. [15] compared several classical and PLM-based methods for a multi-label document classification task with more than 4,000 labels.
By utilizing a label embedding mechanism and multi-task
learning, LAMT_MLC [12] obtained higher performance
than state-of-the-art methods on two large-scale multi-label

classification datasets. On two different question answering
tasks [14], [27], general-domain and domain-specific PLMs
have respectively shown superior performance over other
methods. In the information retrieval field, Shao et al. [28]
developed a system that utilizes both domain-specific pretraining and fine-tuning of BERT [1] and secured one of the

Despite the substantial developments in several aspects
of legal NLP research, the effectiveness of different types
of approaches on a diverse range of legal NLP tasks are
not well understood because they have not been directly
and comprehensively compared in a single study. First of
all, although there exist several NLP benchmarks, such
as GLUE [30], SuperGLUE [31], CLUE [32], KILT [33],
CUGE [34], LexGLUE [35] and GEM [36], most of
them focus on general-domain texts rather than legal data.
Therefore, it is difficult to understand how well existing
approaches work for legal tasks, given the specific challenges
of legal texts as discussed above. Furthermore, existing legal
NLP benchmarks typically have limited coverage on different
types of tasks. For example, Niklaus et al. [22] studied the
effectiveness of simple baselines and several PLMs only on
a binary judgment prediction task. LexGLUE [35] is a recent
effort with broader coverage that evaluated several PLMs on
seven legal datasets; however, six out of the seven datasets
are multi-class/multi-label classification tasks. Also, it only
evaluated general-domain or domain-specific PLMs with no
coverage on state-of-the-art systems.
Considering the challenging nature of legal NLP tasks,
the growing interest in this field and the limited availability and coverage of existing benchmarks, we present
a comprehensive and up-to-date empirical study on eight
datasets across five different legal NLP tasks: binary classification, multi-label classification, multiple choice question answering, summarization and information retrieval.
We start with simple baselines (e.g., unsupervised methods,
classical machine learning algorithms or non-PLM deep
learning approaches) in order to set up the basis for
further comparison. Next, we employ several mainstream
general-domain PLMs on each dataset to examine their
effectiveness over baselines. We further run domain-specific
PLMs (pre-trained on top of general-domain models or from
scratch by using legal documents) on these datasets in order
to study whether PLMs embedded with legal knowledge
can outperform general-domain models. Finally, we apply
six recent state-of-the-art systems that were developed for
general-domain texts to these legal datasets in order to
understand their applicability to the legal domain. By conducting extensive experiments, we try to answer the following
questions:
1) Are simple baseline systems able to achieve decent
performance on legal NLP tasks?
2) Would mainstream general-domain PLMs outperform
simple baselines?
3) Would domain-specific PLMs achieve better performance than general-domain models?

II. RELATED WORK

In this section, we review related techniques for major
NLP tasks in the legal domain. For each task, we discuss
both traditional methods and more recent deep learning/
PLM-based approaches.
A. AN OVERVIEW OF MAJOR LEGAL NLP TASKS

Binary Classification aims at differentiating data samples
between only two classes. Despite its relatively straightforward nature, it has a number of applicable scenarios in the
legal domain, such as legal judgment prediction [37], license
clause fairness classification [13] and overruling statement
detection [14]. SVM [26] and other classical machine
learning algorithms with TF-IDF features are well-suited for
such problems. Different deep learning architectures (e.g.,
LSTM [38] and attention-based methods [39], [40]) with
one-hot encodings of the tokens have also been applied to
binary classification tasks [14], [37], [41].
Researchers have applied PLMs to different legal binary
classification datasets and achieved comparable or better
performance than less complex methods, depending on
the difficulty of the task [14], [22], [37]. For example,
Zheng et al. [14] achieved very similar performance between
SVM and BERT on a simple overruling task, but observed
substantial differences on a more challenging unfairness
detection problem. Due to the imbalanced nature of many
binary classification datasets in the legal domain, class
weights or downsampling techniques may be adopted for
possible performance improvements.
Later in Section IV-A, our experiments show that both
general-domain and legal-specific PLMs are not much better
in terms of F1 score than simple baseline methods on binary
classification tasks where the data is relatively balanced;
however, substantial performance gains can be expected for
more complex and imbalanced datasets.
Multi-class/Multi-label Classification tasks are generally
more complex than binary classifications. Given a document
(e.g., a news article, a legal case, etc.), the goal is to predict
one or more categories for it. Multi-class/multi-label classification techniques have broad applicability to many legal
tasks, such as case outcome extraction [25], legal judgment
prediction [37], document/case categorization [15], [42],
and extracting legal procedural postures [12]. By utilizing
either TF-IDF features [23] or word embeddings [43], [44],
VOLUME 10, 2022

traditional machine learning approaches are readily applicable, including one-vs-all classifiers [43], [45], tree-based
algorithms [46]–[48] and Multilayer Perceptron [49].
Deep learning and PLM-based methods have also been
well-studied for multi-class/multi-label classification tasks.
The organizers of the Chinese AI and Law (CAIL) challenge
applied Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) to two legal
classification tasks and achieved higher accuracy than a SVM
baseline with TF-IDF features [23]. Chalkidis et al. [37]
demonstrated the superior performance of BERT over a SVM
classifier and multiple attention-based approaches on the
ECHR dataset. Song et al. [12] presented a RoBERTa-based
architecture that leverages a label embedding mechanism and
multi-task learning. The system achieved better performance
than both classical machine learning methods and stateof-the-art systems on two legal datasets with 256 and
4,271 labels respectively. Rather than merely relying on
textual information, LeSICiN utilizes a citation network to
identify statutes in legal cases [50]. Using an alternative
approach, Chen et al. [42] mine the most effective terms for
each label from the texts. By using a selected subset of
such label-specific features in a Random Forest classifier, the
system performed substantially better than general-domain
BERT on a multi-class legal case categorization task with
50 labels.
Having a large number of classes and data imbalance
typically result in low performance for multi-label classification tasks. In Section IV-B, we show that although
baselines are able to deal with frequent classes, PLM-based
methods demonstrate much better capability in handling rare
ones. Also, state-of-the-art systems only achieve slightly
better performance than general-domain and domain-specific
PLMs.
Summarization is another challenging task in NLP
research. Given a document, the goal is to produce an
accurate, grammatical, fluent and concise summary that
covers the major aspects of the document. Summarization
systems can be either extractive or abstractive. Extractive
systems typically focus on sentence or word ranking in
order to select the most important parts of the document as
the summary [51]–[53] while abstractive systems normally
involve rewriting and paraphrasing. Many recent systems
follow the Encoder-Decoder architecture [54]. The encoder is
used to produce a vector representation of an input document;
the decoder then utilizes the input states and the vector
representation of the words/tokens that were generated from
previous steps to produce the summary until an EOS token is
generated or it reaches a maximum length. Extractive-thenAbstractive systems have also been proposed: an extractor
first finds the most relevant sentences from a document;
such sentences are then compressed and paraphrased by an
abstractor [55].
Summarization techniques have very practical applications
in the legal domain (e.g., case and legislation summarization),
and both unsupervised and supervised methods have been
adopted in the literature. Listenmaa et al. [56] utilized a

rule-based approach for drafting contracts. CaseSummarizer
is an extractive system that uses both word frequencies and
entities in the sentences for ranking purposes [57]. Hachey
and Grover [58] cast extractive summarization as a sentence
classification problem by leveraging different features, such
as location, length and entities involved. Grabmair et al. [59]
proposed an extractive case summarization system based
on Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). Instead of considering the entire case, it relies on identifying sentences
with higher predictive power with respect to case outcomes.
Rather than summarizing entire cases, LetSum [60] provides
a summary for each major section, such as introduction,
analysis and decision. Duan et al. [61] designed an interesting system that summarizes the debate process in courts
with the goal of effectively assisting judges’ decision-making
process. While not specifically targeting the legal domain,
several recent PLMs have demonstrated promising results
on summarizing U.S. Congressional bills [4], [62]. Two
recent surveys of legal summarization research can be found
here [63], [64]. Although writing summaries for cases or
other legal documents has historically been an editorial process, (semi-)automated summarization systems, especially
those powered by deep learning/PLMs, are promising in
terms of providing effective assistance [65].
Among the general-domain PLMs, our results in
Section IV-C suggest that those specifically pre-trained for
sequence-to-sequence tasks achieve better performance than
others on summarization (or similar) tasks. When using the
base models, state-of-the-art systems show minimal improvements (<1%) over other out-of-the-box methods (e.g.,
BART [3]). One interesting finding is that TextRank [51],
an unsupervised baseline method, outperforms all other
systems on an extremely challenging dataset with long
summaries (182 words on average), suggesting the need for
more advanced approaches to deal with long outputs.
Question Answering generally includes two sub-types:
retrieval-style and multiple-choice. While both have their
respective applications, in this work, we focus on the
latter. Legal question answering has been the focal point
of many studies. Early systems adopted rules and other
unsupervised techniques for answering Yes/No questions
from bar exams [66], [67]. Later works are generally
supervised, using classical machine learning methods (e.g.,
logistic regression) [68] and different types of neural
networks (e.g., CNN) [69], [70]. Following the rise of PLMs,
Zhong et al. compared different approaches on the JEC-QA
dataset (questions from the Chinese bar exam) and showed
that BERT achieved an accuracy of 22% [71]. Tang et al. [72]
achieved a substantially higher accuracy of 28.6% on the
same data by separately modeling evidence/answer and
context/question/answer. However, both systems are far
below a human-level performance of 81%. Huang et al. [73]
presented an interesting study where they jointly model
language generation and question answering, and obtained
better performance on both tasks. Lawformer [74] tries to
specifically address the long-document challenge; however,
75838

it achieved lower performance than a RoBERTa variant. This
suggests that rather than merely feeding more texts to PLMs,
legal question answering may require deeper understanding
and advanced reasoning about the underlying facts and legal
concepts.
Although not specifically designed for legal tasks, several
interesting techniques have been proposed in the literature. MMM [75] uses both out-of-domain and in-domain
datasets during fine-tuning, and outperformed prior systems
on benchmark datasets. Both DCMN+ [76] and BlockSkim [77] try to find the most salient texts rather than having
to process the full context. Based on a similar idea, with
post-hoc interpretation of intermediate results, the system by
Ju et al. [78] penalizes the attributions between the wrong
answers and their supporting text snippets. Instead of only
relying on the given context, Ren et al. [79] tried to retrieve
additional knowledge from an external corpus for context
augmentation. Prioritizing speed, DeFormer [80] processes
the context and the question independently in the lower
layers of PLMs based on the observation that there is less
variance in the lower layer representations of the text when
jointly modeled with different questions. A survey of question
answering research can be found here [81].
In Section IV-D, we show that all PLM-based methods
achieve substantially better performance (∼30% higher
accuracy) than a simple baseline, indicating that question
answering tasks benefit substantially from models that can
capture contextual representations. Furthermore, we achieved
substantially better results using a highly domain-specific
PLM, compared to general-domain PLMs or even a PLM
pre-trained on a legal sub-domain different from the evaluation dataset. The evaluated state-of-the-art system, however,
only obtains similar performance to other PLMs.
Information Retrieval techniques are at the core of many
legal research platforms, such as Lexis+2 and Westlaw
Edge,3 and have several critical application scenarios,
such as legislative document retrieval [82], [83] and case
retrieval [84], [85]. Depending on the application, the queries
can be short (e.g., simple keywords), of medium length
(e.g., Boolean or natural language queries) or long (e.g.,
whole documents). Early legal retrieval systems are generally
unsupervised [86] while recent research tends to center
around different flavors of machine learning techniques [87],
[88]. By using word embeddings, several systems have been
developed to find the relevant sentences within specific
documents [89], [90]. In addition to textual information,
citations [91] and ontologies [92] have also been utilized
for retrieval or legal knowledge extraction. On the query
generation side, Turtle [93] presented a comparison between
Boolean and natural language queries. Locke et al. [94] tried
to generate queries by using the sentences that contain
citations, and compared their effectiveness to manually
crafted ones.

In the context of document-to-document retrieval where
the ‘‘query’’ can be extremely long, Tran et al. [95] first
produce a summary that is further paired with lexical
features in order to retrieve cases. Both PARM [96] and
BERT-PLI [28] also tried to condense the documents, and
performed paragraph-level modeling on top of candidates
returned via BM25 or similar methods. Instead of focusing on
the techniques, Shao et al. [85] presented a comparative user
behavior study between legal and general-domain search, and
suggested that legal information retrieval is more challenging
with respect to query length and number of clicks/pages,
among other metrics. A recent survey on legal information
retrieval is available here [97].
In our work, we focus on the more challenging documentto-document retrieval problem. Here, both the query and the
candidates are full documents and can be extremely long,
thus posing great computational challenges for PLM-based
methods. As shown in Section IV-E, different from the other
tasks, we find that all PLM-based approaches, including a
state-of-the-art system, have substantially lower Micro-F1
scores (5% or more) than an unsupervised baseline algorithm,
suggesting the need for significant further research on legal
information retrieval tasks. A domain-specific PLM, despite
having decent recall, has an especially low precision of
about 6%, indicating that it is unable to differentiate between
similar but irrelevant legal documents.
B. DOMAIN-SPECIFIC PRE-TRAINED LANGUAGE MODELS

Rather than only fine-tuning general-domain PLMs,
researchers have performed domain-specific pre-training
in many domains. SCIBERT [6] comprises a series of
pre-trained PLMs in the domain of scientific publications
(computer science and biomedical research), including
models further pre-trained on top of BERT and those
that were pre-trained from scratch using domain-specific
vocabularies. The pre-trained models demonstrated better
performance than general-domain BERT on named entity
recognition, relation extraction and classification. Similar
efforts have also been made in other domains, such as
BioBERT [7] (biomedical), Med-BERT [98] (medical),
MatBERT [8] (materials science) and GraphCodeBERT [9]
(programming language).
Several legal PLMs have also become available in recent
years. LEGAL-BERT [10] is one of the first efforts and
includes PLMs pre-trained on different types of legal data,
e.g., European legislative documents and U.S./EU case
opinions. Zheng et al. [14] conducted (further) pre-training
with BERT using 3.5 million U.S. cases and demonstrated
the models’ superior performance on both classification
and question answering. Song et al. [12] performed further
pre-training of general-domain RoBERTa; despite using
a much smaller corpus of about 30K U.S. cases, the
system achieved better performance than several state-ofthe-art methods on two large-scale multi-label classification
datasets. In order to tackle the long-document challenge,
Lawformer [74] was pre-trained on a collection of about
VOLUME 10, 2022

23 million Chinese cases with support for up to 4,096 tokens,
and obtained better performance than BERT and RoBERTa
on the LeCaRD case retrieval dataset [99]. In this paper,
we first experiment with several mainstream general-domain
PLMs, comparing their performance to simpler methods.
Furthermore, we study whether domain-specific PLMs would
provide further improvements by evaluating two recent legal
models, AUEB-Eurolex [10] and Custom LegalBERT [14],
which were pre-trained with different types of legal corpora.
C. LEGAL NLP BENCHMARKS

Following the release of general-domain NLP benchmarks, such as SQuAD [100], GLUE [30] and SuperGLUE [31], researchers have made similar efforts in the
legal domain. Several existing works focus on comparing different approaches on specific tasks. For example,
Bhattacharya et al. [101] compared classical and PLM-based
approaches on legal summarization with a focus on judgments from the Indian Supreme Court. In order to build an
Austrian legal knowledge graph, Filtz et al. [102] evaluated
classical techniques (e.g., CRF) and different PLMs on a legal
entity extraction task. Mandal et al. [103] presented another
related effort in catchphrase extraction and explored different
deep learning methods (e.g., CNN and GRU). Without
being task-specific, Chalkidis and Kampas [104] conducted a
thorough evaluation on the effectiveness of word embeddings
on three legal tasks: information extraction, classification and
retrieval.
We compare our study with LexGLUE [35] and
CLAIM [105], which are two recent legal NLP benchmarks.
LexGLUE covers seven datasets for different legal applications, e.g., judgment prediction and legislative document
categorization; however, most of the datasets center around
multi-class/multi-label classification. CLAIM focuses on
Chinese legal documents/cases. The authors compared
classical methods, traditional deep neural networks and
PLMs on several legal applications, such as case matching
and judgment prediction. Compared to LexGLUE, our work
further includes summarization, information retrieval and
binary classification tasks, covering eight datasets across
a total of five different types of NLP tasks (Section III).
In terms of PLMs, CLAIM is mostly centered around BERT
(in addition to other baseline and deep learning methods)
while our work compares a broader range of PLMs (BERT,
RoBERTa, BIGBIRD, T5 and BART). Compared to both
benchmarks, we take the additional step of applying six
recent general-domain state-of-the-art systems to our legal
datasets in order to study their generalizability to other
domains, which is a critical step in further advancing legal
NLP research.

A. DATASET SELECTION

In this paper, we focus on five different types of NLP tasks:
binary classification, multi-label classification, multiple
choice question answering, summarization and information
retrieval. As discussed in Section II, each task has broad
and practical applications in the legal domain. Table 1
summarizes the existing legal datasets for each task and we
highlight the ones used in our study in bold. Although we
include datasets in different languages, this paper focuses on
those in English and we leave PLMs in a multilingual setting
for future work. When selecting datasets, we consider their
size, complexity and also geographical diversity.
1) BINARY CLASSIFICATION

Between the two U.S. datasets, although the Congressional
bill survival prediction dataset [106] has a large number of
samples, we selected Overruling [14] because the underlying
task (deciding whether a given sentence overturns a precedent) could have more significant real-world impact. From
the EU datasets, we chose Terms of Service rather than the
75840

ECHR dataset (cases from the European Court of Human
Rights) [37] because we used two other datasets of court cases
among the remaining tasks. The data by Niklaus et al. [22] is
not in English, thus out of scope.
The Overruling data, although small, represents a very
impactful legal scenario: predicting whether a sentence in a
case contains overruling language. This dataset is relatively
balanced, with positive samples accounting for ∼50% of the
entire dataset. Terms of Service is a larger dataset with ∼9.2K
clauses from online consumer contracts and the task is to
decide whether a given clause is unfair. Each clause was
manually annotated to be clearly fair, potentially unfair or
clearly unfair. Following prior work [13], [14], we consider a
clause to be unfair if it is either potentially or clearly unfair.
Unlike Overruling, this dataset is extremely imbalanced, with
only ∼11% of the clauses labeled as unfair.
2) MULTI-LABEL CLASSIFICATION

Among the multi-label datasets in Table 1, we selected
EUROLEX57K [15] and POSTURE50K [12] for the following reasons. First of all, they are both relatively large
compared to some of the other options and both datasets are
extremely imbalanced. For example, among the 256 labels in

POSTURE50K, only 16 of them occur 500 times or more in
the dataset. Hence, they are ideal for testing PLMs’ capability
of handling rare classes. The documents in EUROLEX57K
and POSTURE50K are also extremely long, with an average
of more than 700 and 2,900 words respectively. Even though
LEDGAR [16] has about 846K provisions (from over 60K
contracts), each provision is only 124 tokens long on average;
therefore, it is less appealing for studying PLMs’ ability to
handle long texts.
POSTURE50K includes 50,000 case opinions between
2013 and 2020 covering all U.S. jurisdictions. The cases
cover a diverse range of areas, such as civil, criminal,
bankruptcy, etc., and are from different courts, e.g., Supreme
Court, Court of Appeals, and trial court. The task is to extract
legal procedural postures, which are motions (e.g., Motion
to Dismiss) brought up by parties and also substantively
discussed in courts. There are 256 different types of motions
with varying frequencies. EUROLEX57K contains 57,000
European legislative documents that cover a variety of topics,
such as environment, finance, transportation, and so on. The
task is to classify each document to a pre-defined set of
4,271 topics, where each document may be assigned more
than one topic. Both datasets present significant challenges
in terms of class imbalance and long texts.
3) SUMMARIZATION

For this task, we selected the JRC-Acquis [18] and the
BillSum [19] datasets for our experiments. We did not find a
download link for the dataset by Bhattacharya et al. [101] and
the LegalOps [20] dataset was not available while we were
conducting our summarization experiments. The AILA2021
dataset and the one by Manor and Li [108] are of substantially
smaller scale than the two selected ones, and are thus less
ideal for our study.
The JRC-Acquis dataset consists of about 23K samples
and the goal is to produce summaries for EU legislation,
international agreements, treaties, among other types of
documents. BillSum also contains about 23K samples, and
the task is to summarize U.S. Congressional and state
bills. One major difference between the two datasets is
their input and output length. In terms of input documents, JRC-Acquis and BillSum have an average of 2,000
and 1,300 words respectively; for the targets, BillSum’s
summaries are 182 words long on average, substantially
longer than JCR-Acquis’ average length of 30 words. The
long inputs from both datasets certainly pose significant
challenges for several mainstream PLMs (e.g., BERT and
RoBERTa where there is a hard limit of 512 tokens); more
importantly, the much longer target texts from BillSum make
the summarization process both more technically challenging
and time-consuming.
4) MULTIPLE CHOICE QUESTION ANSWERING

From Table 1, we selected CaseHOLD [14] for our study
due to its size and recency. Since our work focuses on
publicly available English datasets, JEC-QA [71] and the
VOLUME 10, 2022

one by Adebayo et al. [110] are out of scope. Compared
to CaseHOLD, the Multistate Bar Exam dataset [109] and
PrivacyQA [17] are of much smaller scale with 100 and 1.75K
questions respectively. Furthermore, according to Vold and
Conrad [27], there may also be some data leakage issues in
PrivacyQA. Therefore, we did not select them for our study.
In the CaseHOLD dataset, the task is to find the correct
holding given certain contextual information. A holding is
a court’s answer to a legal issue or question in a case.
To reach a holding, a court looks at the law in question
and applies the facts to that law to decide what the outcome
should be. This outcome is what is referred to as a holding.
Depending on the case, a decision may have one or more
holdings. In CaseHOLD, the holding is removed from a given
paragraph and the task is to choose the correct holding from
five text snippets (i.e., five choices). For each paragraph, there
is one and only one correct holding. Prior work [14], [35]
showed that PLMs achieved accuracies ranging from 70% to
76%, suggesting that it is a non-trivial task and can serve as
a challenging benchmark dataset.
5) INFORMATION RETRIEVAL

Among the several existing English information retrieval
datasets, we selected the one from the 2021-edition COLIEE
competition [21]. COLIEE is a long-running competition in
the legal domain, with the 2021 dataset being particularly
challenging. For example, in the 2020 edition, for each query
(i.e., a whole case), the task is to choose the relevant cases
from a pool of 200 candidates provided by the organizers,
and the proportion of relevant cases is about 2.44%. However,
in the 2021 competition, the number of candidate cases
increased to 4,415 and on average, there are only 5 relevant
cases. Therefore, a system would have to pick out the
5 relevant cases from a much larger pool, a proportion of
about 0.1%. REG-IR [82] is another interesting dataset where
the goal is to map EU directives to UK legislative documents
and vice versa.
We selected COLIEE over REG-IR due to its more
challenging nature (considering the extremely low performances of the participating systems in the 2021 competition) and its potentially broader impact to real-world
applications (e.g., suggest or retrieve case precedents). For
example, according to the REG-IR paper, BM25 was able
to achieve a recall@20 of 45.8% and 87.5% respectively
when trying to retrieve UK legislation for a given EU
document and vice versa; however, as shown later in
Section IV-E, BM25 only obtained a recall@20 of 33.4% on
the COLIEE-2021 dataset. Document-to-document retrieval
is already challenging due to the potential of having
substantial term overlap between ‘‘similar’’ but irrelevant
documents, making it difficult to achieve high precision; the
additional difficulty of finding as many relevant documents
as possible (i.e., obtaining high recall) only makes the
problem even more challenging. Therefore, we selected
the COLIEE-2021 dataset and leave REG-IR for future
work.

B. COMPARISON SYSTEMS

In this paper, we group our approaches into the
following categories: baselines, general-domain PLMs,
domain-specific PLMs and task-specific general-domain
state-of-the-art systems. Table 2 summarizes the systems
that are commonly adopted across the different tasks.
We introduce the state-of-the-art systems while discussing
their results in Section IV.
1) BASELINES

SVM [26] is a classical machine learning algorithm and is
well-suited to binary classification tasks. For both of our
datasets, we performed basic text pre-processing (such as
stopword removal and stemming) and converted the texts to
TF-IDF vectors.
Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) [49] is a simple neural
network consisting of multiple feedforward layers and is
applicable to different types of classification tasks. It is able
to handle multi-label tasks by nature and therefore we apply it
to both multi-label datasets in our study. For MLP, we extract
word-level n-grams to build the feature vectors.
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) [38] is a type of
recurrent neural network and is applicable to both token-level
and sequence-level classification tasks. Generally speaking,
an input sequence of tokens is first converted to a feature
vector via one-hot encoding and embeddings (either a custom
embedding layer or pre-trained embeddings), which is then
sent to a neural network with one or more LSTM layers.
A final dense layer then tries to map the LSTM outputs to
the label space using Softmax or Sigmoid for binary/multiclass or multi-label classification respectively. We adopt
LSTM for both binary and multi-label tasks in this work and
compare its performance to that of classical machine learning
methods.
2) GENERAL-DOMAIN PRE-TRAINED LANGUAGE MODELS

BERT [1] is one of the most widely used general-domain
PLMs. It was pre-trained on two large-scale text corpora
(a total of 16GB uncompressed text) and on two tasks:
masked token prediction and next sentence prediction.
RoBERTa [2], another mainstream general-domain PLM,
has been utilized for a variety of tasks, e.g., classification,
summarization, question answering, and so on. Following
a similar idea to BERT, RoBERTa was pre-trained on over
160GB of uncompressed text with the same two tasks
75842

(but with dynamic masking). In our work, we compare BERT
and RoBERTa to simpler baselines and also set the basis for
further comparison with domain-specific PLMs and state-ofthe-art methods.
BIGBIRD [5] is one of the more recent PLMs. As discussed in Section I, one major challenge in the legal domain
is the presence of long documents, such as legal cases and
legislative documents. BIGBIRD surpasses the 512-token
limit of BERT/RoBERTa and supports up to 4,096 tokens.
Although it seems to be a natural choice for long documents,
we compare its performance to both general-domain and
domain-specific PLMs on three datasets and also discuss
the trade-offs between performance gains and runtime
costs.
BART [3] and T5 [114] are PLMs specifically designed
for sequence-to-sequence tasks. BART was pre-trained using
160GB of news, books, stories and web text. During pretraining, several transformations were applied to the text, e.g.,
token masking and deletion, text infilling (replacing a text
span with a single [MASK] token), sentence permutation and
documentation rotation (by randomly replacing the first token
of the entire document). Also, several different tasks were
involved in pre-training, aiming to support different applications, such as sequence classification, token classification
and generation. T5 also performs span corruption and was
pre-trained with more than 700GB of texts.
3) LEGAL-SPECIFIC PRE-TRAINED LANGUAGE MODELS

Custom LegalBERT [14] is a PLM specifically built for

datasets, especially those that involve legal cases.

C. DATA PREPARATION AND EVALUATION METRICS

Table 3 provides an overview of our evaluation strategy,
including data preparation, evaluation metrics and the model
selection criteria. For the first five datasets in Table 3,
we conduct 10-fold cross validation: We split each dataset
into ten folds of equal size. Each time, 80% of the data is
used for training, 10% is used as validation data and the last
10% is used as test data. We then rotate the folds such that
neither the ten validation sets nor the ten test sets overlap.
For the two multi-label datasets, one unique characteristic
is that many classes only occur once or a few times in
the entire dataset. If we randomly split the data (as in
traditional cross validation), many such rare classes would
fall into the training data without any presence in the
validation or test data, making the task less challenging.
Therefore, instead of doing a traditional 10-fold cross
validation, we performed 10 shuffles to generate 10 different
but overlapping Train/Validation/Test splits by following
the procedure here [12]. The goal of each shuffle is to
generate Train/Validation/Test splits such that the classes in
Validation are a subset of those in Train and Test; at the same
time, classes in Train all have presence in Test. For each
shuffle, we allocate 64% of the data for training, 16% for
validation and the other 20% for testing. The overlapping
rates between different test sets are about 21% and 28%
for POSTURE50K and EUROLEX57K respectively, which
is determined by the number of classes that have sparse
samples in the corresponding datasets. For the COLIEE2021
competition, the original dataset is relatively small, consisting
of 650 training queries and 250 test queries. Therefore,
we shuffled the original training data three times and each
time, 100 queries were randomly selected as the validation
data.
In all situations, for each shuffle, we perform evaluation
on the validation data after each training epoch and select
the best model with the indicated metrics. We follow this
procedure for each parameter setting and use the average
score of the 10 validation sets to select the best setting, and
report the average score of the 10 test sets under that setting.

5) RESULTS OF STATE-OF-THE-ART SYSTEMS

LAMT_MLC was designed specifically to handle rare
classes. It combines domain-specific PLMs and multitask
learning, where one of the tasks targets less frequent
classes in order to boost their signals. It also has a label
embedding mechanism that tries to pull samples of the
same rare classes closer to each other in the embedding
space. Unsurprisingly, it achieved the highest Macro F1
score on both datasets. On POSTURE50K, the differences
in Macro F1 between LAMT_MLC and all other systems
are statistically significant with p-values <0.001, except
for Custom LegalBERT. On EUROLEX57K, although the
differences in Macro F1 between LAMT_MLC and some of
the other systems are relatively small, they are all statistically
significant with p-values <0.001.
We observe a different pattern for LightXML, where it
achieved the best Micro F1 scores on both datasets when
using the corresponding domain-specific PLMs. One of its
core design principles is to make sure the predictions with
the highest confidence scores are correct. For datasets that are
highly imbalanced with a few dominating classes (Figure 2),
it demonstrates advantages on Micro F1 but fails to capture
rare classes, thus adversely impacting its Macro F1 scores.

Test and thus the model does not generalize well. We have
not found a plausible explanation at the moment, but will
continue investigating this.
D. QUESTION ANSWERING
1) PARAMETER TUNING

When using PLMs for multiple choice question answering,
the inputs to the models are the concatenation of the context,
the question and a candidate choice: [CLS] [Tokens in
context (a textual paragraph)] [SEP] [Tokens in a question]9
[Tokens of one of the choices] [SEP]. The [SEP] special
token separates the different parts of the input. We extract
the hidden state of the [CLS] token from the last layer
of a PLM and send it to the classification head, which is
a common practice when using PLMs for multiple choice
question answering. Similar to other experiments, we truncate
the inputs to 512 tokens for all general-domain and domainspecific PLMs and tuned different learning rates ({1e-5,
2e-5}), batch sizes ({8, 16}) and an optional dropout layer
before the classification head (rate ∈ {0.1, 0.2}).
For BiLSTM, we concatenate the textual paragraph with
each of the choices, and truncate the inputs to 275 words.
We then tested different batch sizes ({8, 16}) and learning
rates ({1e-4, 1e-5}). For all experiments, we used the Adam
optimizer and trained for 3 epochs. Instead of an embedding
layer (as in our binary classification experiments), we used
the GloVe embeddings [119].
MMM [75] is a state-of-the-art system that leverages a
novel attention architecture, a two-stage fine-tuning process
and multi-task learning. We did not find the two-stage finetuning code from its repository and also do not have a second
large-scale question answering dataset; thus, our experiments
mainly evaluate the novel attention architecture. For MMM,
due to GPU memory constraints, we used a maximum
input length of 256 tokens and a batch size of 8, and tried
two different learning rates: 1e-5 and 2e-5. We trained all
PLM-based methods for 3 epochs, which we find sufficient
for reaching model convergence.

E. INFORMATION RETRIEVAL
1) PARAMETER TUNING AND RETRIEVAL WORKFLOW

BM25 [120] is an unsupervised scoring method. We first
build an inverted index on all the legal cases in the dataset.
Then, given a query (which is an entire legal case from the
dataset), we return the top-k candidates and experiment with
different values for k ∈ {10, 20, 30, 50}. LMIR [121] models
case retrieval as a query generation process and candidates
are ranked by their probability of generating the input query.
Figure 4 demonstrates the fine-tuning and the inference
pipeline for all PLM models. For fine-tuning (Figure 4a),
the training data consists of all groundtruth case pairs, i.e.,
a case is paired with each of its relevant cases. In order
to obtain negative samples, we use BM25 to retrieve k
candidates and randomly sample m irrelevant cases from
them. Depending on the value of k, the fine-tuning process
may be extremely computationally challenging; thus, we only
use a subsample of the negative cases. Then, we cast this as a
binary classification problem where we try to predict whether
a pair of cases are related or not. Assuming the maximum
input length is n, for a pair of cases, we take n2 tokens from
each case and feed them to the PLMs. The inference process
(Figure 4b) is relatively straightforward, where the query case
is paired with all k candidates and sent to the classifier.

Table 8 presents the results on the COLIEE-2021 Legal Case
Retrieval competition [21]. We performed model selection
and parameter tuning using Macro-F1, which is a typical
practice for information retrieval systems. However, because
the COLIEE2021 competition was judged using Micro-F1,
we also report the Micro scores where each system achieved
its best Macro-F1 score.
One clear but surprising finding is that LMIR
(a classical retrieval method) not only achieved the highest
Micro-F1 score but also substantially outperformed all
other approaches. One possibility is that the contextual
knowledge acquired by the PLMs during pre-training is
negatively impacting their ability to differentiate between
similar but irrelevant cases. This theory is supported by
the especially low precision of all PLM-based methods.
In the COLIEE competition, the task is to find relevant
cases given a query case. Although many of the retrieved
cases may be similar due to substantial term or even topic

overlap, they may not be considered relevant from a legal
perspective.
For example, for query case 072899, RoBERTa, the
best PLM model, returned case 079775 as relevant. Both
cases are about immigration and refugees, thus overlapping
substantially on words and topics; however, they are not
‘‘relevant’’ according to the groundtruth.
This is further worsened by the fact that legal cases may
be (extremely) long, making it very challenging to precisely
spot just a few truly relevant cases. Although BM25 achieved
the highest recall (both Micro and Macro) and MAP when
considering the top-20 candidates, its low precision makes
it impractical for this document-to-document retrieval task
when used alone.
BERT and RoBERTa achieved similar Macro recall; even
though RoBERTa achieved slightly higher precision, both
systems struggle to perform as well as LMIR. Although
BIGBIRD is able to handle longer inputs than RoBERTa,
this does not translate to better performance on this task.

dataset (Table 5), for the purpose of retrieving relevant cases,
merely feeding in more text is less likely to help the model
to better differentiate between similar but irrelevant cases.
Also, we note that the time required to train BIGBIRD was
seven times longer than what was needed for RoBERTa,

V. DISCUSSION, IMPLICATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

In Section I, we proposed five questions that we address in
this study. We summarize our findings in Table 9 and discuss
them in detail in the rest of this section.
A. DISCUSSION OF RESULTS
1) Q1 & Q2: ARE SIMPLE BASELINE SYSTEMS ABLE TO
ACHIEVE DECENT PERFORMANCE ON LEGAL NLP TASKS?
WOULD MAINSTREAM GENERAL-DOMAIN PLMs
OUTPERFORM SIMPLE BASELINES?

According to our experiments, mainstream general-domain
PLMs (and other approaches) generally outperform simple
baseline systems, but the actual improvements may be
dependent on the difficulty of the task. For example, as shown
in Table 4, on the relatively straightforward Overruling
binary classification task, both SVM and BiLSTM achieved
F1-scores of about 94%, only 2%∼3% lower than that of
PLMs; however, on the more complex CaseHOLD question
answering dataset, Table 7 shows that BERT achieved nearly
30% higher accuracy than BiLSTM. One exception is on the
COLIEE-2021 case retrieval task, where baseline systems
75850

actually achieved the best performance. Such retrieval
tasks certainly deserve more attention in future legal NLP
research, especially when dealing with long documents,
in order to better differentiate between similar but irrelevant
candidates.
2) Q3: WOULD DOMAIN-SPECIFIC PLMs ACHIEVE BETTER
PERFORMANCE THAN GENERAL-DOMAIN MODELS?

Based on our results, domain-specific PLMs perform better
than general-domain PLMs on most, but not all, tasks.
Depending on the difficulty of the task, domain-specific
PLMs demonstrate varying levels of benefits. For example,
Custom LegalBERT has a nearly 5% performance gain
over BERT on the CaseHOLD dataset (Table 7); similarly, it achieved about 4% higher Macro F1 score than
BERT/RoBERTa on the POSTURE50K multi-label dataset
(Table 5). However, on the two summarization datasets,
we only observed a small improvement of about 1% when
applying it to the comparatively less challenging JRC-Acquis
data (Table 6a). Although domain-specific PLMs do have
their advantages, they may not universally outperform
general-domain PLMs across the board.
3) Q4: ARE PLM-BASED STATE-OF-THE-ART SYSTEMS
DEVELOPED FOR GENERAL DOMAINS GENERALIZABLE TO
LEGAL DATA?

One unique aspect of our work is to examine the generalizability of the general-domain state-of-the-art systems.
While there are situations where they were able to achieve
better performance, e.g., the JRC-Acquis summarization task
(Table 6a) and the two multi-label classification datasets
(Table 5), the absolute improvements that we observed were
limited. Furthermore, on some of the more challenging
datasets, such as the BillSum summarization data (Table 6b)
and the COLIEE-2021 retrieval task (Table 8), the stateof-the-art systems were either comparable to or even much
worse than the other approaches. Although we only evaluated
six state-of-the-art systems in this paper, they are all from
top-tier conferences/journals and are from the last two years.
Given the observations from our extensive experiments,
we believe that methods that specifically address the unique

In addition to answering these questions, we also discuss
two other observations from our experiments. First of all,
we find that domain-specific PLMs have limited in-domain

cases) and AUEB-Eurolex (pre-trained on EU/UK legislative
documents), and observed improvements when applying
them to datasets that are extremely close to their pretraining corpora, e.g., Custom LegalBERT on POSTURE50K
(Table 5) and CaseHOLD (Table 7) and AUEB-Eurolex on
EUROLEX57K (Table 5). However, when we employ them
to legal datasets that are just ‘‘related’’ to their pre-training
corpora, the benefits of domain-specific pre-training are
generally offset by the language differences across the subdomains. For instance, AUEB-Eurolex only exhibits trivial
improvements on the POSTURE50K multi-label dataset
over BERT (Table 5) and does not have any advantage
on CaseHOLD (Table 7); similarly, Custom LegalBERT
performed even worse than BERT on the EUROLEX57K
multi-label dataset on all three metrics (Table 5). Since
PLM pre-training is expensive with respect to both time and
monetary costs, and may also require special hardware (e.g.,
TPU), only being advantageous on datasets with extremely
similar texts may limit their adoption and prevent them
from having broader impact. One potential future direction
is to improve the in-domain transferability of these PLMs
by utilizing large-scale pre-training corpora that cover a
variety of sub-domains, and by including diverse tasks during
pre-training.
Furthermore, we also discuss the trade-offs between
performance gains and runtime costs for PLMs that are
capable of handling long texts. One unique characteristic
of the legal domain is that the documents may be extremely
long (e.g., as shown in Figure 1 and Figure 3a). This poses
significant challenges especially for PLM-based approaches.
Several mainstream PLMs have a hard input limit of
512 tokens. Although certain PLMs (e.g., BIGBIRD) try
to extend beyond this limit by using different attention
mechanisms, they typically require substantially more computing resources. For example, when applying BIGBIRD
to POSTURE50K (Table 5), a single training epoch took
70 minutes and 120 minutes with maximum input lengths
of 1,024 and 2,048 tokens respectively, while Custom

LegalBERT only needed 15 minutes with 512 tokens. Even
with such substantial runtime differences, BIGBIRD only
achieved about 1.5% higher Micro F1 and Weighted F1
but with a nearly 2% lower Macro F1. In the long run,
domain-specific PLMs may still be preferred, since they
can potentially be used for multiple tasks in the domain,
especially when running multiple downstream fine-tuning
experiments.
B. IMPLICATIONS

When trying to choose the best approach for a given task,
legal NLP researchers and practitioners are often faced with
many different options, including unsupervised methods,
classical machine learning algorithms and deep learning
systems (including those based on PLMs). Considering
the complexity of the task, the capabilities of different
approaches and the time and monetary costs involved, it is
difficult to select the optimal method without practical guidelines obtained via comprehensive empirical evaluation. For
example, PLMs, which are typically more costly than simpler
methods, may not provide substantial performance gains
across all tasks. Furthermore, the release of domain-specific
PLMs may seem promising and a natural solution for almost
any task; however, there is a critical need for legal NLP
practitioners to understand where domain-specific PLMs do
and do not achieve success, as they are not better than
other methods in all cases. To that end, as demonstrated in
Figure 5, we propose a few high-level guidelines for legal
NLP work:
• Domain-specific PLMs can lead to superior performance; however, their pre-training corpora have to
be the same as or extremely close to the evaluation
datasets. The ‘‘Legal’’ domain is very broad and covers
many sub-domains, e.g., case opinions of different
countries and regions, regional legislative documents,
political bills and so on. The languages of documents
in each sub-domain may differ substantially. Our experiments suggest that domain-specific PLMs only provide
benefits when applied to evaluation datasets of the
same sub-domain. When such domain-specific PLMs
are not available, we suggest primarily considering
general-domain PLMs rather than PLMs pre-trained on
corpora of merely ‘‘related’’ domains.
• Based on the architecture in Figure 4 and the results in
Table 8, we recommend starting with classical methods

for document-to-document retrieval tasks, such as LMIR
and BM25, as they are both effective and efficient (i.e.,
no special hardware required). PLM-based methods
could be further considered; however, they may not offer
substantial advantages.
• For the relatively straightforward tasks (e.g., the
Overruling dataset in Table 4), we suggest starting
with simple methods, as they may provide similar
performance to more complex PLM-based approaches
but do not require any special hardware, allowing for less
costly deployment. PLMs may be considered if simple
methods do not provide sufficiently high performance.
• For text summarization, we strongly recommend models
specifically pre-trained for sequence-to-sequence tasks.
For example, in Table 6, both BART and DYPLOC substantially outperformed BERT and Custom LegalBERT
on both of our summarization datasets.
C. FUTURE WORK

In all of our experiments, we either feed entire documents to
PLMs whenever possible or truncate them to some maximum
length. One potential future direction lies in being more
selective about the texts we send to PLMs, i.e., Text Selection.
Take the multi-label POSTURE50K and the case retrieval
COLIEE-2021 datasets as examples. Cases from these
datasets can have thousands of words, but may consist of
multiple ‘‘sections’’ (either explicitly or implicitly) that each
address a different aspect of the case, such as background,
analysis and conclusion. For retrieving relevant cases, the
‘‘summary’’ paragraphs could be more helpful; however,
for the task of extracting legal procedural postures, sections
that provide detailed analyses of the major legal issues
in the case would be needed, since legal postures require
75852

substantive discussion. Therefore, selecting the potentially
most helpful paragraphs or sections for a given task may
lead to better performance. At the same time, this reduces
the need to ingest longer inputs, thus lowering computational
costs.
There have been several studies on fairness, bias and
stereotypes in word embeddings both in the general
domain [122]–[124] and in the legal domain [125]. Similarly,
several recent works have also exposed unintended social
biases in PLMs [126]–[129]. For example, in the context
of legal judgment prediction, Wang et al. [125] showed the
existence of regional sentencing differences on the same
types of crimes. Although not the focus of this paper,
considering the high stakes involved in machine-assisted
decision making in the legal domain and the rising adoption
of PLMs, we believe future research around these topics
deserves more attention from (legal) NLP researchers,
domain experts and social scientists.
Another area of study might be around data annotation.
As mentioned earlier, obtaining high-quality and largescale annotated legal data is expensive in several aspects.
Therefore, it would be interesting to examine how different
techniques (such as active learning and data augmentation)
could facilitate the annotation process, and explore their
impact on final model performance. Finally, given the
existence of datasets in different languages (Table 1),
multilingual PLMs could be another interesting research
topic, especially on the potential transferability across
different languages.
