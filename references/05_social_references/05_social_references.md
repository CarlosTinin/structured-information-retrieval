## 05_01 — A systematic literature review of modern slavery in supply chain management: State of the art, framework development and research opportunities

**Authors:** Vanja Strand, Maryam Lotfi, Anthony Flynn, Helen Walker  
**Journal:** Journal of Cleaner Production (2024), vol. 435, 140301  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2023.140301

### Abstract

Given increased awareness about modern slavery in supply chain management (SCM), this paper undertakes a systematic literature review (SLR) covering 106 published articles. We first carry out a descriptive analysis, with results showing that modern slavery research is on an upward trajectory but suffers from a lack of primary data and theory application. We then extract themes from the published articles and use them to provide theory elaboration of Gold et al. (2015) model of modern slavery in SCM. Specifically, we introduce business culture and high impact risk factors like pandemics as new institutional factors; highlight external stakeholders like recruitment agents and audit firms as integral to understanding the business context of modern slavery; and add prevention and remediation to detection and response as discrete categories in managing modern slavery risks. We also discuss the gaps in modern slavery research in supply chains. Finally, we propose that reshoring, industry 4.0 and supply chain collaboration represent SCM specialisms that can inform modern slavery research going forward.

### Introduction

Modern slavery has received increasing attention in recent years, across a range of stakeholders such as businesses, non-government organisations (NGOs), policy makers, and academics. High profile modern slavery cases have heightened public awareness. There has been a raft of legislation in the last decade, with California being the first US State to introduce the 'Transparency in Supply Chains' Act (2010), followed by the UK Modern Slavery Act (2015), Australia Modern Slavery Act (2018) and EU regulations banning products made with forced labour.

There has also been a spike in academic interest in modern slavery, much driven by the recent availability of corporate statements on modern slavery. The Covid-19 pandemic exposed modern slavery risks in the supply chains of PPE, apparel and other manufactured products.

This SLR aims to provide a comprehensive review of modern slavery in supply chains literature by searching a broader range of journals and updating and extending Gold et al.'s (2015) early framework. The study makes three contributions: (1) extending recent reviews by using Scopus and Web of Science to explore research across law, human rights, business ethics, operations management, and SCM fields, covering 106 articles since 2010; (2) building on Gold et al.'s (2015) conceptual model with new insights; (3) identifying three contemporary SCM research streams (reshoring, industry 4.0, supply chain collaboration) that can inform modern slavery research.

The definition adopted extends Gold et al. (2015): "the recruitment and subsequent exploitation of a person (or a person in chattel or debt bondage slavery), who is deprived of their individual liberty anywhere along the product, human or labour supply chain to the final customer for service provision or production."

### Conclusion

This study conducted a systematic literature review on modern slavery in SCM, provided an updated definition, and elaborated upon Gold et al.'s (2015) model. We introduce new factors to the model, highlight additional external stakeholders, and extend detection and response by adding prevention and remediation as discrete phases in managing modern slavery risks.

Limitations include: this SLR is a secondary data-based study; it focuses on academic journal articles in English published after 2010 in Scopus or Web of Science; the selection process may have been too restrictive.

Implications for future research: gaps in the revised conceptual model could serve as starting points; stakeholder theory lens was not applied; institutional theory could help explain organisational adaptations to modern slavery risks. Modern slavery researchers can learn from methodological techniques that elicit primary data on sensitive subjects. There is a need to represent perspectives of lower tier suppliers and voices of people with lived experiences. More quantitative research using mathematical modelling approaches from data science would be beneficial to explore relationships between factors influencing modern slavery in supply chains.
