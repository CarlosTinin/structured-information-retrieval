## 05_01 — A systematic literature review of modern slavery in supply chain management: State of the art, framework development and research opportunities

**Authors:** Vanja Strand, Maryam Lotfi, Anthony Flynn, Helen Walker  
**Journal:** Journal of Cleaner Production (2024), vol. 435, 140301  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2023.140301

Strand et al. conduct a comprehensive systematic literature review of 106 peer-reviewed articles on modern slavery in supply chain management (SCM), published between 2010 and 2023, sourced from Scopus and Web of Science across multiple disciplines (law, human rights, business ethics, operations management, and SCM). The review employs Seuring and Müller's (2008) four-step SLR methodology (material collection, descriptive analysis, category identification, material evaluation) and uses Gold et al.'s (2015) seminal framework on modern slavery in SCM as the theoretical starting point for theory elaboration.

The descriptive analysis reveals that modern slavery research in SCM is on an upward trajectory (with nearly half of all articles appearing in 2020–2021) but suffers from critical methodological limitations: an over-reliance on secondary data (particularly corporate modern slavery statements), insufficient application of theory (only 30 of 106 articles use a theoretical framework), and geographic concentration on the UK, US, and Australia — countries with enabling legislation rather than high slavery prevalence. Industry-specific research focuses predominantly on agriculture, forestry, fishing, and textiles manufacturing — labor-intensive sectors with known high risk.

The paper's central contribution is the elaboration of Gold et al.'s (2015) model through the introduction of: (1) business culture and high-impact risk factors (e.g., pandemics) as new institutional-level factors; (2) external stakeholders (recruitment agents, audit firms) as integral to the corporate-stakeholder context; and (3) prevention and remediation as discrete management phases alongside detection and response. The authors adopt an extended definition of modern slavery that includes the human/labour supply chain (recruitment pathway) beyond the product supply chain.

Three contemporary SCM research streams are proposed as future directions: reshoring (as a potential strategy to reduce exploitation in offshore supply chains), industry 4.0 technologies (IoT, AI, blockchain for supply chain visibility), and supply chain collaboration (multi-stakeholder approaches to address systemic risks). The paper explicitly calls for more primary data collection, quantitative mathematical modelling, and representation of lower-tier supplier perspectives and lived experiences of exploitation — gaps that are directly relevant to the present framework's goal of using AI-extracted judicial data to complement existing supply chain transparency sources such as the "dirty list" (lista suja).
