ABSTRACT
Official gazettes are documents published by governments to publicize their actions, spanning long periods of
time and making an important transparency mechanism. These documents have information on laws, contracts,
and bidding processes, as well as on civil servants and their careers in public service. Automatic information
extraction of these documents may contribute to public transparency, with two tasks being especially useful: the
classification of the different segments of these documents, the so called acts; and the Named Entity Recognition
(NER) within the acts. The variety of official gazettes and their patterns brings up the necessity of constructing
different tools for specific gazettes. In this paper, we propose DODFMiner, a command-line interface tool to
classify acts and extract named entities from the Official Gazette of the Federal District. The tool follows a
3-step approach: the pre-processing of the input data; text classification using rule-based systems with regular
expressions; and NER with Machine Learning algorithms. It allows users to input JSON files and receive CSV
as output, providing information that allows users to track government procurements through years, contracts
duration and total amount, among others. We also propose a set of experiments to support the choice of
models included in the tool, covering the classification and NER steps. Text classification achieved a mean
F1-score of 0.778, while to the NER, we compared 3 different architectures, CRF with a mean F1-score of
0.851, CNN-biLSTM-CRF with 0.787 and CNN-CNN-LSTM with 0.841.

1. Introduction
Official gazettes are periodical publications that aim to publicize
governments legal actions, having information on contracts, civil servants, procurements, published laws, among others. They are widely
used worldwide as recorded by the Foreign Official Gazette Database,1
counting up to 663 active publications in 2007. These are periodical
publications that frequently cover large periods of time, with notable
examples being The London Gazette, published in the United Kingdom
since 1665, and the Buletín Oficial Del Estado, since 1661 in Spain. In
Brazil, the Official Diary of the Union (ODU) has been published since
1862 and all the federative units have their respective official gazettes,
making a total of 27 publications. Municipalities can also have their

own gazettes, such as is the case of the cities São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro,
Feira de Santana, among others.
They constitute an important source of information to the society,
and have also historical importance due to the periodicity of the
publication. The right of public access to legal information makes
governments responsible for publishing legal information online in an
accessible way [1]. Providing data in an accessible way empowers
citizens to build their own tools to track government’s actions, being
able, for example, to track governments and politicians’ spending to
identify irregularities [2].
In Brazil, the Federal District has its own official gazette, the Official
Gazette of the Federal District (OGFD).2 It is composed of a series of
reports of government actions and is published on a nearly daily basis.
OGFDs contain information about retirements, public procurements,

The acts are composed of different named entities, which are text

people’s names, dates, etc [3]. The entities appear inside the text,
with varying positions and patterns. This leads us to the Named Entity
Recognition (NER) problem, part of the field of Natural Language
Processing (NLP) that deals with the identification and classification
of entities in texts [4–6]. Besides manual approaches, which are costly
and tedious, basic approaches can be used to tackle it, such as using
hand-crafted regular expressions based on the textual patterns [7]. This
process can perform well in many cases, but due to the specificities of
the OGFD documents, which vary according to time and their writer,
it is not completely reliable to extract named entities here, requiring
frequent updates.
Machine Learning (ML) techniques, however, can learn patterns
and be used to deal with NER. A common architecture used in this
task is the Conditional Random Field (CRF) [8], often used with deep
learning systems such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) [9] and
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) [10]. CRF aims to maximize the
conditional probability of observation and state sequences, based on
feature functions [11]. ML models have the advantage of being able to
generalize the process of a given task, thus making them more resistant
to format changes.
To deal with these problems and to extract information from the
OGFDs documents, we propose DODFMiner, an automated tool that
can perform NER on OGFD documents in 3 steps: (𝑖) with the segmentation of the documents; (𝑖𝑖) the classification of these acts; (𝑖𝑖𝑖)
and the recognition step, where the named entities are recognized and
classified. Each one of these steps presents specific contributions.
In the segmentation step, the documents are split into acts after preprocessing, using a rule-based system. The pre-processing deals with
some HTML tags that may come within the text and also JSON objects
that come with more than one act within it, separated into different
paragraphs. In the classification step, we propose a set of regular
expressions to identify multiple document types that deal with contracts, amendments, procurements, among others. These expressions
were validated through experiments. Finally, with the NER step we
conducted a set of experiments to extract named entities from official
gazettes, including information like names, number of processes and
contracts, contract objects, contract values, duration of agreements,
among many others.

2.1. Official gazette of the Federal District
Official gazettes are governmental publications where acts with
public decisions are issued. They constitute an important source of information to the general public because they have information such as
procurements released, accepted civil servants, laws and legal decisions
in force, among others. Since they are published almost daily and with
regularity over a long set of time, these documents also constitute a
rich source of historical information to the citizens.
In Brazil, ODU is the federal official gazette, published daily since
1862, constituting more than 150 years of public information on the Executive, the Legislative and the Judiciary branches. It is the main source
of information on the Brazilian Federal Government [12]. Besides that,
all of the 27 Brazilian federative units have their corresponding official
gazettes, as well as some cities. This results in a great variety and
diversity between these gazettes, with different patterns being adopted.
As a consequence of that variety, the gazettes can have a different
number of sections, each one having different types of legal acts. The
way the information is publicized also does not follow a single flow:
while the Imprensa Nacional (National Press) provides a website where
the ODU documents can be found in text format and searched through
an engine, some official gazettes are published only in PDF format,
without any tool to facilitate the information extraction.
The Federal District Government publishes its official gazette, the
OGFD, since its foundation in 1960. Editions published during the first
seven years are not available on the Internet, and the editions published
between October 1967 and April 2020 can be downloaded only in PDF
format, without any segmentation between the different topics in the
document. Since May 2020, OGFDs can be found downloaded in JSON
format, divided by segments, called acts.3
The OGFDs are split into three sections: Section 1 covers acts
from the Judiciary and Legislative; Section 2 covers acts related to
civil servants and public companies; and Section 3 includes agreement
and contract extracts, contract amendments, trial verdicts, and other
matters related to private legal entities. Since the purpose of the tool
is monitoring administrative activities, only acts from Sections 2 and 3
are the focus of this work.
The Sections 2 and 3 are further divided into subsections according
to the issuing agency. The acts are displayed in a sequential manner
through paragraphs within each subsection, often with a single paragraph for each act. Moreover, acts of the same type are usually grouped
together. Furthermore, the amount of information published daily can
vary based on the administrative work.

Pinto et al. (2021) [14] also proposed a tool to extract named

web crawler, and then processed them into text format. Using regular

ML techniques was chosen in a more complex scenario like NER. In
order to ensure the performance, experiments with regular expressions
and different ML techniques were conducted. It can extract classified
segments, as well as the named entities within them. Additionally,
the DODFMiner offers a library that can be used by developers in
specialized applications.

2.3. Text classification and named entity recognition
Text classification is the task of attributing a predefined label to
a given text, assuming categorical values for the labels [18]. It is an
important NLP task used in different areas such as news filtering, document organization, sentiment analysis and e-mail classification [19].
The NER task can be viewed as an extension of the text classification
problem for dealing with context-sensitive systems. That is, while the
standard text classification problem considers its elements to be independent for the task, the context is always considered to be relevant in
NER.
Text classifiers can be separated into different categories depending
on the techniques used. Rule-based approaches result in classifiers modeled on a set of rules, where a condition is made on a feature set, with
the class label being the result of the conditions being satisfied [19].
Regular expression classifiers fit in here, where rules are specified to the
set of possible input strings [18], giving them a label if the conditions
are satisfied. Regular expressions are sometimes sufficient for simple
tasks where the object of classification follows a well-defined pattern.
A second approach to classifiers is through statistical methods.
Classical approaches result in classifiers based on feature extraction
techniques such as Principal Component Analysis, Based Discriminant
Analysis and Average Neighborhood Margin Maximization, being inefficient for large quantities of data [20]. Modern approaches include
ML algorithms, where classifiers are trained and tested through labeled data, based on supervised learning, semi-supervised learning
and unsupervised learning. Different techniques such as Naive Bayes

2.2. Information extraction from gazettes
Knowledge extraction from official gazettes is not a new topic, and
some approaches to the problem can be found in the literature. While
some of these papers also introduce an extraction tool, not all tools are
accompanied by a paper, and thus may go undocumented. Additionally,
almost none of the tools found in our research used ML techniques to
find entities, and instead, regular expressions seemed to be the most
common method [13,14].
Cação et al. (2022) [15] proposed an approach to collect and classify
the ODU documents, resulting in the Government Actions Tracker
(GAT), a data set used by tools such as the Public Acts Monitor,4
where the data can be easily investigated through a user interface. The
data pipeline is composed of different steps: a web scraper gets the
XML documents; a rule-based bot classifies the most interesting acts,
which are sent to a team of experts to detect errors; and the classified
documents go to the GAT data set and are used to train ML models.
The documents have annotations regarding Action, Circumstance and
Classification, focusing on environment-related decisions.

Classifiers [21], Logistic Regression [22], Support Vector Machine
(SVM) [23] and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) [24] fit in this group.
The ANNs can still be classified into different groups: shallow learning and deep learning approaches [25]. The shallow approaches succeeded the rule-based approaches, being composed of neural networks
with few hidden layers and showing higher accuracy and stability [25].
Deep learning approaches have more complex networks, with more
hidden layers, being able to model features without the need of feature engineering. State-of-the-art text classification algorithms are deep
learning models, specially Transformer-based language models [25].
According to Jing et al. (2020) [3], ML architectures such as CRFs
and Hidden Markov Models have been widely applied to problems
related to the NER task. CRFs, in particular, have been extensively used
with deep learning models in more complex tasks. However, rule-based
systems with hand-crafted rules are still used, usually achieving high
precision and low recall.
In contrast to traditional models such as SVMs [23], CRFs are able
to consider the context of an element when classifying it [3]. This
property can be useful when dealing with sequences where the order
of its components is meaningful, such as texts. While CRFs are often
incorporated with deep learning structures [26,27], the base CRF can
still be useful in multiple applications [28].
In the last few years, deep learning techniques have been reaching
state-of-the-art performance in the task of entity recognition. A common method is to use word embeddings to encode the text, and feed
this data to a structure such as a LSTM or CNN. These structures can
also be concatenated to improve the depth of processing, and the CRF
is sometimes added to the end.
Entity recognition is typically performed by labeling each word in
a format such as IOB, IOBES or ConLL [29]. In the IOBES format, for
instance, the first word of an entity has the B (Beginning) label, while
the last word has the E (End) label. Words inside an entity have the I
(Inside) label, and those outsides of any entities have the O (Outside)
label. Finally, the S (Single) label is used for entities that have a single
word. The evaluation of performance in NER tasks can be done through
the metrics precision, recall, and F1-score [3]. Precision is used for
analyzing the number of false positives, and recall is used for analyzing
the number of false negatives. F1-score is defined as the harmonic mean
of precision and recall.
The DODFMiner tool explores a combination of rule-based and ML
systems. A rule-based system was made to tackle the text classification problem, using regular expressions. The regular expressions were
manually constructed and validated through trial and error, based on
readings of the documents. The NER problem, however, was tackled
with ML techniques. Different models were trained and analyzed to
choose the model that fitted the tool’s requirements the most. The
models were trained with two high-quality corpora, and the chosen one
presented the simplest and more accurate solution.

2.4. Related NER tools
Different NER tools are already available to use, offering opensource solutions to identify and extract entities from texts. The opensource software library spaCy5 offers a set of NLP tools, including the
EntityRecognizer. It allows users to create a NER pipeline using
pre-trained models or to train a new custom model based on labeled
data [30]. It provides a simple and powerful solution to tackle the
NER problem, but it requires users to configure it. Another tool related
to NER is the T-NER,6 which focuses on finetuning and testing NER
models. It allows fine-tuning of NER models through 2 stages, where
first every possible configuration is tested for a small epoch, and then
the top-K models are selected to continue fine-tuning until the L epoch,
with K and L being specified by the user. It also provides a set of
pre-trained models which are already fine-tuned [31].
There are still libraries that provide a suite of tools related to NLP,
such as NLTK [32]. It provides a simple system to detect named entities
in texts given as input, as well as algorithms to train custom entities
not available within their default NER solution. However, the default
named entities available are still very limited, not using ML models and
not dealing with text segmentation.
We can highlight some major differences between the DODFMiner
and these other tools: our solution comprises text segmentation through
REGEX and NER through ML models, not only NER; we focus on
official gazettes, specially those published in Brazil, contributing to
the legal document information extraction field; and we offer a user
ready solution, where there is no need to configure a pipeline or
models. However, our tool can benefit in the future from some features
from these other tools, specially the flexibility to include custom and
state-of-the-art models, such as Transformers.

In the second step, the segmented acts are classified into 18 types
(12 related to Section 2 and 6 related to Section 3). These types are
more specific than the typography already provided in the JSON files
and are related to the possible named entities. The classification is done
through a rule-based system, where regular expressions are given as
conditions and the texts are labeled if they satisfy those conditions.
The regular expressions can be seen in Appendix B. Each act type is
implemented as a specific class in the tool, inheriting from the same
base act type. Within these classes, the regular expressions are searched
and the acts are classified.
3.3. Named entity recognition
Finally, the entity recognition module is used to identify and extract
named entities from the acts. This module receives a list of acts from
the previous step as input. These entities can be identified through a
CRF model. The model was chosen based on the experiments detailed
in Section 4. Additionally, the types of entities that can be extracted
vary according to the act type. The entities of all OGFDs specified
for extraction are combined into standardized tables, one for each
act type, facilitating the search of information through multiple documents. Fig. 2 shows a segmented Reversal act with its named entities
highlighted.

The DODFMiner can be installed through the Python Package Index
(PyPi).8 It can also be downloaded as a Python package through the
public GitHub repository, besides the CLI version.9 A detailed documentation on how to use and contribute to the public repository was
made with the help of the Read the Docs platform in English.10
The DODFMiner can be used as a CLI program. After installing it, the
extraction can be done using the command below. The flags required
are described in the following command:

It receives a JSON file as input, which is pre-processed in a step
where acts are segmented from the documents. These acts serve as input
in the document classification step, where regular expression rules are
used to classify the different act types. The classified texts go to the NER
step, where CRF models extract the named entities within the texts. The
named entities are then put in a CSV file that contains all the identified
entities from the types specified by the user. The following subsections
discuss these steps.

The command extract indicates to the tool that the extraction
will be performed. The -s flag indicates the path, and it can indicate
a path to a folder containing JSON files or a specific JSON file. When
a folder is put in here, the tool extracts all the information and put it
in the same output file. The flag -a indicates the types to be extracted,
being followed by the id of the type. The ids can be seen in Tables A.5
and A.6. When no id is specified after -a, all the available types are
extracted.
Table 1 shows an example of a resulting table based on the CSV
file with named entities information. This table contains a Reversal
act entities extracted from a specific OGFD. Entities with sensitive
information were removed from the table.

3.1. Pre-processing and segmentation
The tool receives as input a JSON file, downloaded by the user in the
OGFD website. The detailed structure of the file can be seen in Listing
Section 3.1, an example of a segmented Reversal act. The texts of the
documents are in the INFO object, where the 3 sections are divided
into different objects. Within the sections, there is still the division
based on the agency that issued the documents, which are displayed as
nested objects within them. The acts’ objects also have metadata that
is not used by the tool, such as the number of the act and a general
classification which do not represent the type of act. Only the text of
these objects is extracted and used by the tool.
The acts within these JSON documents are provided partially segmented. Section 3 acts, regarding contracts and procurements, are
already segmented, but Section 2 acts have multiple documents of the
same type grouped in one text, separated by paragraphs. In this step,
the paragraphs are also split to segment the documents. By the end of
it, the segmented acts are provided as output to be used by the next
step of the tool.

4.2. Text classification
Regular expressions were written to every one of the 18 act types
analyzed in this tool, based on the reading of the documents, which
allowed us to manually detect patterns within the text. The expressions
can be seen in Tables B.7 and B.8. To validate these expressions, we
used the Personnel Acts Corpus and the Contracts and Procurements
Corpus, gold standard corpora manually annotated on OGFD documents and which provide labels on document segmentation, document
classification and NER.
In the validation step, we used all of the annotated acts of these
corpora and separated the regular expressions between two groups:
the expressions related to acts published in Section 2; and the ones
published in Section 3. Every regular expression was applied to all of
the acts published in its section, giving a binary metric to validate,
e.g. it was correctly classified or not.
With 15 of the act types, the classification with the regular expressions was made simply by using them to search the texts, classifying them if the expression was found. However, Contract Extract,
Agreement Extract and Contract Amendment required more complex
searches, with specific expressions used first in the title of the act and
then, if the search was successful, another search within the text to
assure that the classification was right.
Table 3 shows the validation metrics of the experiments conducted
with regular expressions to classify the documents. The lowest F1-score
was achieved by the Dismissal of Effective Position type, with 0.51,
due to its lack of clear patterns. All the other 17 types achieved F1scores higher than 0.62. However, some discrepancies can be found in
here, since we also have types such as Substitution, with a score of
0.98. This discrepancy can be explained by how easily recognizable are
the patterns of each type, which is also related to the quantity of the
documents published.

4.1. Data sets
The OGFD documents are composed of three sections, with the tool
focusing on Sections 2 and 3. A different data set for each section
was used for the experiments, both manually annotated. These corpora
made possible the use of ML techniques, since they deal specifically
with OGFD documents, as well as the evaluation of the regular expression technique used here. Table 2 details the number of labeled
instances in each data set.
Section 2 has 12 different act types, while Section 3 has 6 act
types. The annotation was done in three steps: the segmentation, where
every act was identified within the documents; the classification of the
segments; and the named entity recognition of the classified acts. The
classification and the NER data of the data sets were used here.
It is worth noting that the data sets are not balanced, with some
types having much more acts than others. While Nomination of Commissioned Position has 2.313 annotated documents, Rendered Ineffective Retirement has only 20. There is also a similarity between some

CNN-CNN-LSTM and CNN-biLSTM-CRF were chosen in here as our candidates for the model to be used within the tool. A hold-out validation
was done, separating the datasets in train and test sets, to calculate
precision, recall and F1-score.
The CRF model was generated using sklearn-crfsuite library11 and the
lbfgs algorithm, with the following basic input features: (𝑖) the word in
lower case; (𝑖𝑖) whether the word is a title (i.e. if the first letter is in
upper case and the others not); (𝑖𝑖𝑖) whether the word is all in upper
case; (𝑖𝑣) number of digits in the word. The final features of a word are
these 4 basic features concatenated with the features of the 4 nearest
words on the left and 4 on the right, as well as its relative position
within the text and a bias feature.
The CNN-CNN-LSTM [33] architecture used 50 channels in the first
CNN to create character-level embeddings, 800 channels in the second
CNN to create word-level embeddings and a size of 200 to the hidden
LSTM layer. A pre-trained GloVe embedding with 100 dimensions [34]
was also used, with the help of the Adam optimization algorithm. A
fixed learning rate of 1.5 ∗ 10−3 was used, with 100 epochs. A similar
configuration was used to the CNN-biLSTM-CRF [35] architecture, with
the exception of the word-level embeddings, which were not used.
A hold-out validation was used, separating the data sets between
20% of validation data and 80% of training data. Every act type was
trained and validated using only the data set that contains it. The

used to calculate the averages of each act type.
The results of the NER models can be seen in Table 4, where

The results show a similarity between the CRF and the CNN-CNNLSTM weighted F1-scores: both achieved better F1-scores in 7 act
types, tying in Dismissal of Commissioned Position with a score of
0.95. The CNN-biLSTM-CRF model outperformed the others only in
3 cases. However, the CNN-CNN-LSTM showed some low F1-scores
when compared to the CRF in 3 types: Contract Amendment, with
0.374 against 0.863 in the CRF; Procurement Suspension, with 0.389
against 0.765; and Procurement Notice, with 0.545 against 0.858. This
difference cannot be justified by the average support in these types,
since Contract Amendment and Procurement Notice have large average
support, with 2904 and 1821, respectively. The discrepancy between
the CRF and CNN-CNN-LSTM is smaller when the second outperforms
the first one, with the biggest difference happening with the Reversal
type, where CNN-CNN-LSTM achieved 0.916 and CRF 0.787.

Fig. 4 shows the average recall and precision obtained by each
model for each entity type. It shows that the recall and precision were
similar in most cases, with CRF and CNN-CNN-LSTM models achieving
higher results. The CNN-biLSTM-CRF models obtained lower recall and
precision, indicating a large quantity of false positive and false negative
classifications. The CRF also had more entity types with high precision
and low recall than the CNN-CNN-LSTM, indicating that these models
return fewer results but also fewer false negatives.
The validation of the models guided us in the decision of which
algorithm to use in the tool. The CNN-biLSTM-CRF algorithm showed
the worst results, with large quantities of false positives and false
negatives, being easily discarded from our options. CRF and CNN-CNNLSTM, however, showed similar results, with some types performing
better in one or the other. The most evident differences occurred with
Procurement Suspension (0.765 with CRF and 0.398 with CNN-CNNLSTM), Procurement Notice (0.858 and 0.545) and Contract Amendment (0.863 and 0.374), in which the CRF performed better. Since these
models showed similar results, we chose to use the CRF because of its
simplicity and easiness of incorporation into the tool.

5. Conclusion
The main contribution of this paper is the CLI tool to automatically extract classified texts and named entities from OGFDs using
ML algorithms, as well as rule-based systems. DODFMiner constitutes
as valuable tool to improve the transparency of the Brazilian Federal
District government decisions, providing more detailed information
than simple web crawlers on these gazettes could.
DODFMiner can also help the development of other tools, since it
allows fast and detailed information extraction from OGFDs. Derived
tools may include: detailed search engines that can output segmented
and classified documents, as well as being able to filter texts based on

Future work to be done includes improving the CLI tool, as well as
the experiments made. A web crawler could be included in the tool
to download documents published in a period defined by the user.
The tool could also be improved to include PDF extraction and information extracting, allowing users to analyze older OGFD documents.
The classification step might benefit from state-of-the-art deep learning
algorithms, instead of using rule-based systems as it is. And statistical
tests should be applied to the NER experiments, with 𝑘-fold training,
allowing us to find whether there is a significant difference between
the models used instead of just using the mean F1-score to choose a
model, as it is now with the CRF.

The tool follows a 3-step approach, with pre-processing and segmentation, text classification and NER. Experiments were conducted to
validate these steps, with classification achieving a mean F1-score of
0.83 using a rule-based system. NER experiments were conducted with
CRF, with a mean F1-score of 0.851, CNN-biLSTM-CRF, with 0.787, and
CNN-CNN-LSTM, with 0.841. Due to its simplicity and high accuracy,
the CRF was chosen to compose the NER step.
